## CLAIM 1 — Kramer et al. 2012, max R² = 0.81 — **VERIFIED**

**Source read (R):** Europe PMC REST API record for DOI 10.1021/jm300131x (publisher-supplied abstract, retrieved raw JSON, not a summary). Full text is PAYWALLED at ACS (`isOpenAccess = N`); **abstract only**.

**Verbatim final two sentences of the abstract (R):**
> "The experimental uncertainty is estimated to yield a mean error of 0.44 pK(i) units, a standard deviation of 0.54 pK(i) units, and a median error of 0.34 pK(i) units. The maximum possible squared Pearson correlation coefficient (R(2)) on large data sets is estimated to be 0.81."

Also verbatim from the same abstract (R), useful framing:
> "The maximum achievable accuracy of in silico models depends on the quality of the experimental data. Consequently, experimental uncertainty defines a natural upper limit to the predictive performance possible. Models that yield errors smaller than the experimental uncertainty are necessarily overtrained."

**Full citation (R):** Kramer C, Kalliokoski T, Gedeck P, Vulpetti A. The experimental uncertainty of heterogeneous public K(i) data. *J Med Chem*. 2012;55(11):5165–5173. DOI 10.1021/jm300131x. PMID 22643060. Peer-reviewed.

**Caveats (I):** The claimed sentence is in the abstract verbatim — this is a primary-source verification, not second-hand. But the *derivation* of 0.81 (what "large data sets" means, what pKi variance was assumed) is in the paywalled body and is **NOT VERIFIED**. If the paper cites 0.81 as a ceiling it should be attributed as "estimated for heterogeneous public Ki data with SD 0.54 pKi units", not as a universal ceiling.

## CLAIM 2 — Krüger & Overington 2012, non-normality test + Laplace fit — **VERIFIED, but the previous agent's framing is WRONG**

**Source read (R):** Full text XML, Europe PMC `PMC3257267/fullTextXML` (open access, raw, not summarized).

**Full citation (R):** Krüger FA, Overington JP. Global analysis of small molecule binding to related protein targets. *PLoS Comput Biol*. 2012;8(1):e1002333. DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002333. PMCID PMC3257267. Peer-reviewed, open access.

**(1) The non-normality test — verbatim (R), Results:**
> "We calculated the difference in bioactivity for each pair of orthologs and a given ligand. The density distribution of differences in bioactivity has a central peak at 0 and is non-normal as established by Anderson-Darling test [24] (p<2e-16)."

**(2) The Laplace fit — verbatim (R), Results, "Evolutionary relationship and small molecule binding":**
> "An overlay of the distributions of differences in binding affinity for both, human to rat orthologs and human paralogs is shown in figure 2. Both distributions can be approximately described by a Laplace distribution. Notably, the scale parameter b, which describes the spread of the distribution, is b = 0.7 for the paralogs and b = 1.3 for human-to-rat orthologs (as expected by the differing selective pressures within these two sets)."

**(3) Fitted parameters (R):** Laplace scale b = 1.3 (human-to-rat orthologs), b = 0.7 (human paralogs). No location parameter, no goodness-of-fit statistic, and no fitting method is reported — "approximately described by" is the only qualifier given.

**(4) Sample sizes — verbatim (R):**
> "we retrieved 2,782 instances of compounds tested against both, the human target and its ortholog in rat. The human-to-rat data is made up of 151 different orthologous pairs."

> "Activities of 20,309 compounds against 516 different human targets were obtained and 651 pairs of human paralogs identified, and differences in binding affinity were calculated for a total of 41,733 combinations."

Separate inter-assay control set — verbatim (R):
> "As a measure of both publication and abstraction errors within the ChEMBL database and also for between-lab variability, and to estimate the deviations introduced by inter-assay comparison of binding data, we calculated the differences in bioactivity (expressed in terms of a log pKi) between assays that measured the effect of identical ligands on identical targets. To ensure the resulting distribution was comparable to the distribution of differences in activity between human-to-rat orthologs, this distribution was constructed as a composite of 1.500 randomly picked inter-assay differences for human targets and 1.500 randomly picked inter-assay differences for rat targets. […] The resulting distribution closely resembles the distribution for orthologs"

**(5) Heavy-tail / non-Gaussian statement:** **NOT PRESENT.** Verified by exhaustive keyword count over the full text (C): "Laplace" appears **1** time, "Anderson" **1** time, "normal" **1** time (the sentence quoted above), and "Gauss", "heavy", "tail", "kurtos" appear **0** times. The paper never uses the words heavy-tailed, leptokurtic, or Gaussian.

**⚠️ CRITICAL CORRECTION (I) — do not cite this as an error-distribution result.** Both the Anderson-Darling test and the Laplace fit are applied to the distribution of **differences in binding affinity between two related protein targets** (human vs rat ortholog; human paralog pairs). That quantity is biological divergence *plus* measurement error, not measurement error. The paper's own pure-measurement-error proxy is the *separate* inter-assay distribution (same ligand, same target, different assays; n = 3,000). For that distribution the paper reports **no normality test and no Laplace fit** — only the qualitative statement that it "closely resembles the distribution for orthologs." So:
- "Krüger & Overington tested the error distribution for non-normality and fitted a Laplace" → **overstated, would be a misattribution in your paper.**
- Defensible wording (I): "Krüger & Overington found that differences in measured binding affinity between related targets are non-normal (Anderson-Darling, p<2e-16) and approximately Laplace-distributed (b = 1.3 for human–rat orthologs, b = 0.7 for human paralogs), and that a control distribution of purely inter-assay differences for identical ligand–target pairs closely resembles it."

That last clause is the bridge that lets you use it as heavy-tailed-noise support, and it is quoted verbatim above — but it is the authors' qualitative claim, not a fitted result.

### Sub-claim (c): "MSE improvements on average around 20% on assays above 35% censoring" — **VERIFIED**

Verbatim (R), Results:
> "Proportionally, the enhancements of the MSE are still, on average, around 20% for these models on the assays with > 35% censored labels. The combined results of these models in terms of MSE and NLL, indicate that the inclusion of censored labels during the training procedure mostly impacts the uncertainty estimation abilities of the models rather than predictive accuracy."

Scope restriction (R): this "around 20%" applies to **Ensemble, MC-Dropout and Bayes by Backprop only**. The paper immediately states the opposite for the Gaussian models (R):
> "On the contrary, the predictive accuracy of the Gaussian models trained with the Tobit loss is mostly not improved by including the censored labels. […] The proportional losses in MSE are comparable to the enhancements in NLL, consisting of around −30% on average for all assays. This is an unfortunate drawback of our proposed adaption of the Gaussian models using the Tobit loss"

**(I) So "ignoring censoring worsens uncertainty estimates" is directionally right but the flat "~20% MSE improvement" must be qualified by model family — quoting it unqualified would misrepresent the paper, which reports a −30% MSE penalty for the Gaussian models.**

Abstract, verbatim (R):
> "Our results demonstrate that despite the partial information available in censored labels, they are essential to reliably estimate uncertainties in real pharmaceutical settings where approximately one-third or more of experimental labels are censored."

Conclusion, verbatim (R):
> "The enhancements are especially prominent when the proportion of censored labels makes up roughly one-third or more of all available data for a given assay."
