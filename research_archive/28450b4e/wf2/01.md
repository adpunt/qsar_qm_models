## 1. Kolmar SS & Grulke CM (2021) *J Cheminform* 13:92, doi:10.1186/s13321-021-00571-7

Read in full from `/Users/apunt/Downloads/13321_2021_Article_571.pdf`.

### 1a. How they scale noise — **fraction of the endpoint's observed RANGE** [READ]

Section "Random error generation", p. 6, verbatim:

> "Random error was added to datasets by sampling from a Gaussian distribution of zero mean and increasing standard deviation σ_noise. Noise was added only to the target variables and not to the descriptors. **This σ_noise was determined from the product of the range of endpoint values in the dataset, the noise level n, and a multiplier. This multiplier was set to 0.01 after experimentation with a range of values and observing the effect on RMSE.** Each dataset was used to generate 15 noise levels with 5 replicates at each noise level. Because n starts at 0, the 0th noise level has no added noise."

Equations 7 and 8 verbatim (subscripts flattened by the PDF extractor):

> (7)  `Y_noise_n,i = Y + N(0, σ_noise_n)`
> (8)  `σ_noise_n = (Y_max − Y_min) * multiplier * n`,  `n ∈ (0, …, 14)`,  `i ∈ (1, …, 5)`

So: **σ = 0.01 · n · (endpoint range)**, n = 0…14. Top noise level is therefore **14% of the endpoint range** [COMPUTED from Eq. 8]. Ranges are given per dataset in their Table 1 (e.g. Lip/logD −1.5 to 4.5; G298_atom −2417 to −288 kcal mol⁻¹).

### 1b. How they normalise their axes — **both axes divided by RMSE₀** [READ]

p. 9, verbatim:

> "In order to compare trends in data across algorithm and dataset, we chose to **normalize the RMSE and the amount of added noise by RMSE₀, which is the RMSE obtained from training and predicting on the original noiseless dataset. In the top subplot, the y-axis is RMSE/RMSE₀. The x-axis is the standard deviation of the Gaussian distribution from which the added error was sampled (σ), divided by RMSE₀.** Therefore, the y-axis indicates the multiplicative increase in observed prediction error, while the x-axis is most accurately understood as **the fractional amount of error inserted into the dataset standardized by the amount of prediction error seen in the noiseless dataset.** In this figure, a constant value of 1 on the y-axis would corresponds to seeing the exact same error at a particular noise level as was seen when modeling the native dataset. Similarly, a line through the origin with a slope of 1 would represent the expected RMSE obtained if one compared Test_noise to Test_true."

Their summary statistics are the two slopes: m_noise = slope of RMSE_noise/RMSE₀ vs σ/RMSE₀, and m_true = slope of RMSE_true/RMSE₀ vs σ/RMSE₀ [READ, p. 10].

Note the raw-axis figures (Figs 3, 4) plot RMSE and R² against **σ in the endpoint's own units** [READ, Fig. 3 caption: "the x-axis is the standard deviation (σ) of the Gaussian distribution of the added error"]. The RMSE₀-normalisation is used only for the cross-dataset comparison.

### 1c. How they anchor to real assay error — one sentence, one endpoint [READ]

p. 13, verbatim, in full:

> "Additionally, it is useful to contextualize the amount of simulated error which has been added to these datasets within what is known about experimental uncertainties. **Estimates for most of the endpoints used in this study are not readily available**, however Kramer, Kalliokoski and colleagues found from an examination of the ChemBL database that heterogeneous pIC50 data has an average standard deviation of 0.68 log units [19]. For the BACE dataset, which uses a pIC50 endpoint, **1.1 log units of noise were added, or 1.6 times the average standard deviation reported in ChemBL**."

That is the entire anchoring argument: for the *one* dataset whose endpoint has a published error estimate, they express their top injected σ as a **multiple of the published assay SD** (1.1 / 0.68 = 1.6×). They explicitly concede no such anchor exists for the other seven datasets.

They also state for QM data [READ, p. 5]: *"quantum mechanical calculations do not have random experimental error, because the same calculation will give exactly the same number [28]"* — directly relevant to your QM9 set.

### How THEY parameterised noise magnitude — **variance scaled to the pIC50 RANGE** [READ, abstract, Europe PMC]

> "Model performance on the test set was used as a proxy to monitor the relative noise sensitivity of these algorithms as a function of the level of simulated noise added to the bioactivities from the training set. **The noise was simulated by sampling from Gaussian distributions with increasingly larger variances, which ranged from zero to the range of pIC50 values comprised in a given data set.** General trends were identified by designing a full-factorial experiment, which was analyzed with a normal linear model."

So the top noise level has **variance** (not SD) equal to the endpoint range — i.e. σ_max = √(range). Same family as Kolmar (range-referenced) but on the variance scale, which makes them non-comparable without care. **[INFERENCE]**

While the datasets showed a diversity of targets, the type of endpoint, pIC50, is the same for each dataset. Additionally, **the range of estimated native experimental error for these datasets is only 1.1 log units.**"* — this is a **secondary** account and should be attributed as such.

**NOT VERIFIED:** the exact 10-level grid (whether linear in variance or SD, and the exact values), and whether noise went into the test set. Kolmar says test-set RMSE stayed constant while noise was added *to the training set* [READ, Kolmar p. 10], implying **train-only** injection.

## 4. Broader ML label-noise literature for regression

The honest finding: **the label-noise literature is overwhelmingly classification, where the standard parameter is the noise RATE (fraction of flipped labels), not a magnitude.** Frénay B & Verleysen M (2014) "Classification in the Presence of Label Noise: A Survey", *IEEE TNNLS* — already in your `citations.bib` line 1765 — is the canonical survey and is titled for classification. For regression there is no single accepted convention. [INFERENCE, supported by the search results below.]

The most defensible regression-side convention I can cite with a verbatim primary source is **signal-to-noise ratio**:

Hastie T, Tibshirani R, Tibshirani R, "Best Subset, Forward Stepwise, or Lasso? Analyses and Recommendations Based on Extensive Comparisons", arXiv:1707.08692 (published *Statistical Science* 2020, 35(4):579–592). Verbatim from §2.2 "What is a realistic signal-to-noise ratio?" [READ via ar5iv]:

> "Let (x₀, y₀) ∈ ℝᵖ × ℝ be a pair of predictor and response variables, and define f(x₀) = 𝔼(y₀|x₀) and ε₀ = y₀ − f(x₀), so that we may express the relationship between x₀, y₀ as: y₀ = f(x₀) + ε₀. **The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in this model is defined as SNR = Var(f(x₀)) / Var(ε₀).**"
> "PVE(f) = 1 − Var(ε₀)/Var(y₀) = SNR/(1 + SNR)."

That paper is an extended argument that **the SNR axis is the right axis** — that method rankings reverse across the SNR range and comparisons at a single unstated SNR are misleading. That is exactly your confounding problem, one level up. [INFERENCE about applicability; the SNR-reversal finding itself is READ from the abstract: *"neither best subset selection nor the lasso uniformly dominate the other, with best subset selection generally performing better in high signal-to-noise (SNR) ratio regimes, and the lasso better in low SNR regimes"*.]

Two further conventions I saw only in **secondary search summaries**, not verified in primary text — treat as **NOT VERIFIED**: σ = κ·σ_targets with κ ∈ (0,1) (attributed to arXiv:2607.08103); and the SRSD symbolic-regression benchmark noise formula (arXiv:2206.10540 — I fetched the abstract and it does **not** contain the formula).

For the **shape** side, the relevant classical framework is Huber's ε-contamination / gross-error model, F = (1−ε)Φ + εH — Huber PJ (1964), "Robust Estimation of a Location Parameter", *Ann. Math. Statist.* 35:73–101. This is the textbook formalisation of your "Outlier" strategy: **two knobs, a contamination fraction ε and a contaminant scale**, which is precisely why a single σ cannot control it. I have this from **secondary sources only** (search summaries and citing arXiv papers); I did not read Huber 1964 itself — **NOT VERIFIED verbatim.**

# ANSWERS

## A) Fairest way to express "how much noise" so different noise SHAPES are comparable

**Use the realised root-mean-square perturbation actually delivered to the labels:**

**D = √( (1/N) Σᵢ 𝔼[εᵢ²] ) = √( (1/N) Σᵢ σᵢ² )**  (for zero-mean noise)

Report it in the endpoint's own units, and make it the x-axis. The knob you turn (`sigma`) is an implementation detail; **D is the dose.** Two strategies are comparable only at equal D.

Who uses the concept, with citations:

- **Kolmar & Grulke 2021** put the delivered σ on the x-axis, not a level index — *"the x-axis is the standard deviation (σ) of the Gaussian distribution of the added error"* [READ, Fig. 3 caption]. With a single Gaussian shape, delivered σ ≡ D, so they never had to name the distinction. You do.
- **Zhao et al. 2017** likewise define k against a physical SD, so their k is a delivered dose, not an index [READ].
- **Huber's ε-contamination model** (Huber 1964) is the formal statement that a contaminated-noise law needs *two* parameters — mixing fraction and contaminant scale — so no single knob can hold the dose fixed across shapes [NOT VERIFIED verbatim; from secondary sources].

The second-moment match is the right invariant because RMSE and R² — your outcome metrics — are second-moment functionals. It deliberately does **not** equalise shape (kurtosis, per-molecule σ spread), and that is the point: **shape is the treatment, dose is the thing you hold constant.** [INFERENCE]

Report alongside D, per strategy and per σ, at least: the per-molecule σᵢ spread (max/min) and the fraction of molecules receiving the high-σ branch. Your existing figures already imply spreads of 1× (Gaussian, Threshold) up to 20–30× (Quantile, Outlier), which is the shape signal you actually want to attribute.

## B) Fairest way to express noise magnitude across DATASETS (eV vs log units)

**Two axes, both reported, because they answer different questions:**

**B1 — the primary cross-dataset axis: SNR, or equivalently noise variance as a fraction of label variance.**

SNR = Var(f(x))/Var(ε), PVE = SNR/(1+SNR) — Hastie, Tibshirani & Tibshirani, arXiv:1707.08692 / *Statistical Science* 2020 [READ, verbatim in §4]. Unitless, so eV and log units land on one axis; and their whole paper is a demonstration that method rankings *reverse* along this axis, which is the argument for making it your axis. In practice, report **D/SD(y)** — the injected RMS dose over the dataset's label SD — which is the same quantity in a form directly comparable to the published QSAR convention.

**Precedent for exactly this normaliser:** Zhao, Wang, Sedykh & Zhu 2017 [READ]: *"the standard deviation of each data set was multiplied by a parameter k (k = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0), and this result was denoted as sigma."* That is D/SD(y) = k. **This is the closest thing QSAR has to a standard, and it is the one I would build the paper on.**

**Do not** use fraction-of-range as the primary axis. Kolmar (Eq. 8) and Cortés-Ciriano (abstract) both do, but range is a two-order-statistic quantity — one outlier moves it, and QM9's HOMO–LUMO gap range is set by a handful of molecules. Mention it as prior art; don't adopt it.

**B2 — the anchoring axis, for the three experimental sets only: absolute log units.**

Your topic file's conclusion is right and I found nothing to overturn it: **assay error in log units is roughly constant across these endpoints regardless of label spread, so a fixed σ in log units is more defensible for the "is this realistic?" claim than an SD-matched σ.** Report D in log units next to D/SD(y).

**QM9 is a special case and must be worded separately.** Kolmar states it outright [READ, p. 5]: *"quantum mechanical calculations do not have random experimental error, because the same calculation will give exactly the same number."* For QM9 you have no assay error to anchor to; D/SD(y) is the only honest axis there, and the eV figure is a controlled perturbation, not a simulated measurement.

## C) Exact recipe: solve for a shape's internal parameters so realised noise hits a target

Read from `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/rust/src/main.rs`, `generate_adaptive_noise` (L294–420) and the CLI defaults (L1160–1225).

**Setup.** Every strategy is a per-molecule scale map σᵢ = s · gᵢ(y), where `s` is the CLI `sigma` and gᵢ is the strategy's shape function — verified: all six branches multiply the passed `sigma` and none re-normalise. Noise is drawn εᵢ ~ Dist(0, σᵢ), so 𝔼[εᵢ²] = σᵢ².

**Realised dose at s = 1** (call it the shape's unit dose):

**G = √( (1/N) Σᵢ gᵢ(yᵢ)² )**

**Realised dose at any s:  D(s) = s · G**   (exact — the shape map is linear in s)

**Solve for the target τ:  s\* = τ / G**

That is the whole recipe. Compute G once per (dataset split, strategy, strategy-params) from the **training labels only** — the same labels the noise map is built from — and rescale.

**Closed forms for your six shapes** (defaults from L1175–1219):

1. **Legacy / Gaussian** — σᵢ = s.
  **G = 1** → s\* = τ.

2. **Threshold** (`high_threshold` h = 1.0, `low_threshold` l = −1.0; multipliers 2.0 / 0.1) — σᵢ = 2s if yᵢ ≥ h or yᵢ ≤ l, else 0.1s. Let p = (1/N)·#{yᵢ ≥ h or yᵢ ≤ l}.
  **G = √(4p + 0.01(1−p))** → s\* = τ / G.
  For QM9 gap (min 2.08 eV > 1.0), p = 1 → G = 2 → **s\* = τ/2**.

3. **Quantile** (q_hi = 0.9, q_lo = 0.1; multipliers 2.0 / 2.0 / 0.1) — tails get 2s, middle 0.1s. By construction p ≈ 0.2.
  **G = √(4p + 0.01(1−p))** = √(0.808) = **0.899** → s\* = τ/0.899.

4. **Outlier-focused** (z_thr = 2.0; multipliers 3.0 / 0.1) — σᵢ = 3s if |zᵢ| > 2 else 0.1s. Let p = (1/N)·#{|zᵢ| > 2}.
  **G = √(9p + 0.01(1−p))** → s\* = τ / G.

5. **Value-proportional** (`base_sigma` = s, f = 0.1) — σᵢ = s(1 + f|yᵢ|).
  **G = √( (1/N) Σᵢ (1 + f|yᵢ|)² ) = √(1 + 2f·μ₁ + f²·μ₂)**, with μ₁ = mean|y|, μ₂ = mean(y²).
  → s\* = τ / G.

6. **Heteroscedastic** (α = 0.1s², β = 0.05s²) — σᵢ² = s²(0.1 + 0.05|yᵢ|).
  **G = √(0.1 + 0.05·μ₁)** → s\* = τ / G.

**Cross-check that these formulas are right [COMPUTED].** Applied to QM9 HOMO–LUMO gap, they reproduce the RMS doses at σ=1 already recorded in your project notes:
- Threshold: G = 2 → 2.000 ✓ (notes: 2.000)
- Quantile: G = √0.808 = 0.899 ✓ (notes: 0.899)
- Outlier: notes give 0.502 → inverting, 0.502² = 9p + 0.01(1−p) → **p = 2.69%**, a plausible >2σ tail fraction ✓
- ValProp: notes give 1.701 → inverting with f = 0.1 gives μ₁ ≈ 7.0 eV
- Hetero: notes give 0.669 → inverting, 0.669² = 0.1 + 0.05μ₁ → **μ₁ = 6.95 eV**
The last two independently recover the *same* mean |gap| from two different shape functions. That is a strong internal consistency check that the algebra above matches the shipped Rust code.

**Two mandatory caveats.**

- **Threshold is degenerate on QM9 and matching won't fix it.** Your notes verify min gap = 2.08 eV > h = 1.0 eV, so 100% of molecules take the high branch and Threshold *is* Gaussian at 2×. After rescaling to equal D, **Threshold becomes numerically identical to Gaussian on QM9** — six strategies collapse to five. Say so in the paper rather than letting a reviewer find it; or move the cut-points into the label distribution (e.g. ±1 SD from the mean) so the strategy does something.
