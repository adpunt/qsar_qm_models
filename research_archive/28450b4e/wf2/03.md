### 1. Inter-laboratory / inter-assay scatter in public bioactivity data

**S1. Kramer C, Kalliokoski T, Gedeck P, Vulpetti A (2012). "The Experimental Uncertainty of Heterogeneous Public K(i) Data." *J Med Chem* 55(11):5165–5173. DOI 10.1021/jm300131x** (abstract read verbatim from PubMed; full text paywalled — NOT READ)
- Measured: reproducibility of independent pKi measurements of the same compound–target pair in ChEMBL.
- Quote: *"The experimental uncertainty is estimated to yield a mean error of 0.44 pK(i) units, a standard deviation of 0.54 pK(i) units, and a median error of 0.34 pK(i) units. The maximum possible squared Pearson correlation coefficient (R(2)) on large data sets is estimated to be 0.81."*
- Quote (data-quality mechanism): *"ChEMBL contains unit-transcription errors, undifferentiated stereoisomers, and repeated citations of single measurements (90% of all pairs)."* ← 90% of naive "replicate" pairs are not replicates at all. That is a *structured* database artefact, not noise.

**S2. Kalliokoski T, Kramer C, Vulpetti A, Gedeck P (2013). "Comparability of Mixed IC50 Data — A Statistical Analysis." *PLoS ONE* 8(4):e61007. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0061007** (full text downloaded and read locally)
- N after filtering (their filter table, read verbatim): *"Remove pairs with overlapping authors 3.480 [systems] 10.895 [IC50 data points]"* (European thousands separators → 3,480 systems, 10,895 points).
- Random-scatter magnitude: *"σ pIC50 = 0.68, MUE pIC50 = 0.55 and MedUE pIC50 = 0.43"*; Gaussian fits to the inner distribution: *"Upper threshold 1.5 2.0 2.5 / ΔpIC50 σ = 0.80 σ = 0.84 σ = 0.86 / ΔpKi σ = 0.66 σ = 0.68 σ = 0.68"*.
- Practical translation, verbatim: *"A standard deviation of 0.68 corresponds to a factor of 4.8, meaning that 68.2% of all IC50 measurements agree within a factor of 4.8, even when measured in different laboratories under potentially different assay conditions."*
- **SYSTEMATIC OFFSET (this is the key non-random finding):** *"On average, the measured pKi values are 0.355 log units larger than the measured pIC50 values, corresponding to a factor of 2.3."* And: *"After subtracting 0.35 log units from the pKi values and correcting by √2, pKi and pIC50 values agree with an R2 = 0.46, σ = 0.68, MUE = 0.54 and MedUE = 0.43."* → a **constant additive bias tied to assay type**, which they explicitly recommend correcting.
- Same-lab, same-assay control compounds: *"The standard deviation of the pIC50 values are σ = 0.22 for rolipram/PDE4D and σ = 0.17 for cilostamide/PDE3."*
- Same lab, same species, still bad: *"Although the assays have been run in the same lab on DHFR from the same species, the IC50 values of rattus norvegicus DHFR agree with R2 = 0.25 and MUE = 0.61."*

### 2. Batch / plate / positional effects in HTS

**S8. Malo N, Hanley JA, Cerquozzi S, Pelletier J, Nadon R (2006). "Statistical practice in high-throughput screening data analysis." *Nat Biotechnol* 24(2):167–175. DOI 10.1038/nbt1186** (full PDF downloaded and read locally)
- This is a **review**; it states the error model but reports no population-level magnitude.
- The model, verbatim: *"Another simple model is Yijp = µijp + Rip + Cjp + εijp where R and C represent plate-specific row and column artifacts, respectively, and εijp represents remaining sources of error."* Contrast with the naive model they reject: *"Assuming no bias, the εijk's are assumed to have zero mean and a specified probability distribution (e.g., normal)."* ← **this is exactly your six strategies, and this paper explicitly says it is the wrong model for HTS.**
- Mechanisms, verbatim: *"Other factors ... include potency differences across compounds, and systematic across-plate and within-plate column or row biases (e.g., edge effects)."*; *"Cell-based biological controls are especially problematic because of variable growth patterns; cell clumping or evaporation within different areas of the plate can lead to different growth conditions and ultimately to position-related bias."*
- Amplification via controls: *"Unfortunately, a systematic outer column effect affects all of the measurements on the plate because they are adjusted relative to these controls."*; *"Worse still, the edge effects may be present in some plates but not others."*
- Their empirical demonstration (Figure 4): *"Figure 4 shows a strong and variable column effect for screen 295."* (variance-ratio box plots; no scalar magnitude given in text).

**S9. Dragiev P, Nadon R, Makarenkov V (2011). "Systematic error detection in experimental high-throughput screening." *BMC Bioinformatics* 12:25. DOI 10.1186/1471-2105-12-25** (PMC3034671 full text downloaded and read locally)
- Real assay: *"McMaster Test assay consists of 50,000 different chemical compounds ... two copies of each of the 625 plates were run through the HTS equipment; 1250 plates in total."*
- Detection result, verbatim: *"All three tests detected the presence of systematic error in both surfaces for both considered confidence levels α = 0.01 and 0.1."*
- **Magnitude of consequence, verbatim:** *"If raw HTS data are not treated properly for eliminating the effect of systematic error, then many (e.g., about 30% of hits in the case of McMaster Test assay, as reported in Table 5) of the selected hits may be due to the presence of systematic error and, at the same time, many promising compounds may be missed during hit selection."*
- Their Table 5 (read): at μ−3SD threshold, Well Correction changed the hit list from **96 original hits → 26 hits, 70 removed, 0 added**. At μ−2.29SD: **96 → 102, with 24 removed and 30 added.** The hit list is not robust to removing the positional bias.
- Mechanisms listed verbatim: *"robotic failures and reader effects, pipette malfunction or other liquid handling anomalies, unintended differences in compound concentrations due to agent evaporation or variation in the incubation time and temperature differences, and lighting or air flow present over the course of the entire screen."*

**S10. Caraus I, Alsuwailem AA, Nadon R, Makarenkov V (2015). "Detecting and overcoming systematic bias in high-throughput screening technologies: a comprehensive review of practical issues and methodological solutions." *Brief Bioinform* 16(6):974–986. DOI 10.1093/bib/bbv004** — ⚠️ **paywalled; quotes below were returned by an automated fetch of the OUP page and matched on two independent fetches, but I could not open the PDF to confirm surrounding context.**

### 3. Structure-dependent error

**S11. Feng BY, Simeonov A, Jadhav A, Babaoglu K, Inglese J, Shoichet BK, Austin CP (2007). "A High-Throughput Screen for Aggregation-Based Inhibition in a Large Compound Library." *J Med Chem* 50(10):2385–2390. DOI 10.1021/jm061317y** (full PDF downloaded and read locally)
- N: *"we tested 70 563 molecules from the National Institutes of Health Chemical Genomics Center (NCGC) library for detergent-sensitive inhibition."*
- Prevalence: *"Overall, 1204 molecules (1.7%) met both the inhibition and the curve criteria and were identified as aggregation-based inhibitors, whereas only 70 (0.1%) were identified as detergent-insensitive."*
- **Fraction of the *signal* that is artefact:** *"A striking result of this screen is that 95% of the actives can be attributed to a single one of these: aggregate-based inhibition."*
- Condition-dependence (i.e. the "error" is not a property of the molecule alone): *"This previous study suggested that as many as 19% of 298 randomly selected drug-like molecules behave as aggregators at 30 µM."* and *"At least a 2-fold difference in potency was observed for most aggregators tested under both conditions."*
- Extrapolation to other screens (their own inference, flagged as such by them): *"If this pattern extends to the other enzyme screens, it suggests that, despite the use of detergents and other adjuvants, more than 20% the actives in these screens are also aggregators."*

**S12. Irwin JJ, Duan D, Torosyan H, Doak AK, Ziebart KT, Sterling T, Tumanian G, Shoichet BK (2015). "An Aggregation Advisor for Ligand Discovery." *J Med Chem* 58(17):7076–7087. DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01105** (abstract read verbatim from PubMed; body quotes via automated fetch of PMC4646424)
- **Error is predictable from structure + a physicochemical property:** *"80% of these molecules had cLogP values of >3, and we adopted this as a criterion for predicting aggregation"*; the advisor flags a compound when *"calculated LogP values >3 and Tc ≥ 85%"* to a known aggregator.
- Prospective validation (abstract, verbatim): *"five of seven new molecules with Tanimoto coefficients (Tc's) between 0.95 and 0.99 to known aggregators aggregated at relevant concentrations. Ten of 19 with Tc's between 0.94 and 0.90 and three of seven with Tc's between 0.89 and 0.85 also aggregated."*
- Prevalence in the literature: *"61 827 or 5.1% of the ligands acting in the 0.1 to 10 μM range in the medicinal chemistry literature are at least 85% similar to a known aggregator with these physical properties and may aggregate at relevant concentrations. Intriguingly, only 0.73% of all drug-like commercially available compounds resemble the known aggregators, suggesting that colloidal aggregators are enriched in the literature. As a percentage of the literature, aggregator-like compounds have increased 9-fold since 1995."*
- **This is the single cleanest published demonstration that measurement error is selected by structure and by lipophilicity, and that the contaminated fraction is enriched in exactly the databases QSAR uses.**

**S13. Baell JB, Holloway GA (2010). "New substructure filters for removal of pan assay interference compounds (PAINS) from screening libraries and for their exclusion in bioassays." *J Med Chem* 53(7):2719–2740. DOI 10.1021/jm901137j** (abstract read verbatim)
- Structure-defined interference: *"This report describes a number of substructural features which can help to identify compounds that appear as frequent hitters (promiscuous compounds) in many biochemical high throughput screens."*
- **The abstract contains no prevalence numbers.** Any "% of libraries flagged as PAINS" figure is NOT VERIFIED by me.

**S14. Jasial S, Hu Y, Bajorath J (2017). "How Frequently Are Pan Assay Interference Compounds Active? ..." *J Med Chem.* DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.7b00154** — **SECONDARY / summary only, full text NOT READ.** Summary states >23,000 extensively tested PAINS-substructure compounds analysed, *"median values of two to five hits for PAINS tested in hundreds of assays"*, and that the same PAINS substructure occurs in both consistently inactive and frequently active compounds. **Report this as the counterweight**: PAINS membership alone is a weak predictor of measured-activity error. Do not present PAINS as a large quantified error mechanism.

**S15. Avdeef A (2019). "Multi-lab intrinsic solubility measurement reproducibility in CheqSol and shake-flask methods." *ADMET & DMPK* 7(3):210–219. DOI 10.5599/admet.698** (PMC8957238; quotes via automated fetch)
- *"SD_iALL = 0.17 log unit, has been estimated from the 870 molecules for which solubility was reported from two or more different sources"*.
- Method-dependent: *"The average interlaboratory standard deviation (SDi) is slightly better for the CheqSol set than that of the SSF set: SDiCS = 0.15 and SDiSSF = 0.24"* (124 overlapping compounds).
- **Inter-lab ≫ intra-lab, verbatim:** *"The intralaboratory errors reported in the CheqSol method (0.05 log) need to be multiplied by a factor of 3 to match the expected interlaboratory errors for the method."* → the between-lab component is ~3× the within-lab component. A single zero-mean i.i.d. σ cannot represent both.

**S16. Llinàs A, Avdeef A (2019). "Solubility Challenge Revisited after Ten Years, with Multilab Shake-Flask Data, Using Tight (SD ∼ 0.17 log) and Loose (SD ∼ 0.62 log) Test Sets." *J Chem Inf Model* 59(6):3036–…** (title read verbatim from the ACS listing; **DOI NOT VERIFIED**, full text NOT READ)
- The **title itself is the finding**: the authors partitioned 32 test compounds into a *tight* set with inter-lab SD ≈ 0.17 log and a *loose* set with inter-lab SD ≈ 0.62 log — a **3.6× difference in measurement error between two groups of molecules in the same endpoint**, assigned by compound identity, not by solubility value. Secondary note from a search summary (unverified): test set 2 comprises *"contentious"* molecules with higher average measurement uncertainty.

**S17. Takács-Novák K, Avdeef A (1996). "Interlaboratory study of log P determination by shake-flask and potentiometric methods." *J Pharm Biomed Anal* 14(11):1405–1413. DOI 10.1016/0731-7085(96)01773-6** (abstract read verbatim)
- N = 23 compounds, 2 labs. *"The interlaboratory evaluation of pKa and log P data obtained by potentiometry showed excellent agreement (average delta pKa = +/-0.02 and delta log P = +/-0.07)."* Between methods over ~8 log units: *"log PpH-metric = 0.9794 log Pshake-flask -0.0397 (r = 0.9987, s = +/-0.091)"*.
- **INFERENCE (mine, flagged):** the fitted slope 0.9794 ≠ 1 and intercept −0.0397 ≠ 0 describe a *slope-and-intercept* (multiplicative + additive) method bias, not zero-mean noise — but with r=0.9987 the effect is tiny here. Do not oversell this one; for a carefully-run pair of physicochemical labs the systematic component is small. This is the **negative control** in your evidence set.

### 5. Dablander et al. 2023 — what the local PDF actually says about noise

## B) WHAT ZERO-MEAN INDEPENDENT PER-LABEL NOISE CANNOT CAPTURE

Being specific, per mechanism:

1. **Censoring is not zero-mean and not symmetric.** A right-censored label recorded as an exact number has expectation strictly *below* the truth. Adding N(0,σ) to a label can never produce a one-sided, truncated, threshold-dependent error. Your six strategies all preserve E[y_noisy] = y. Real ChEMBL/industrial data does not. (S18)
2. **Correlated blocks.** Plate/lab/assay error is shared by *groups* of compounds: Y = μ + R_i + C_j + ε (S8, verbatim). Independent per-label noise has zero covariance between molecules by construction; batch error has a rank-1-per-block covariance. Consequence: model bias, not just variance inflation — and a model can *learn* the batch offset if batch identity leaks through structure.
3. **A constant additive shift on a subpopulation.** The +0.355 pKi-vs-pIC50 offset (S2) is a mean shift on a subset. No zero-mean strategy produces it.
4. **Selection by structure rather than by label.** Aggregation is predicted by cLogP>3 plus Tanimoto ≥0.85 to a known aggregator (S12). Your six strategies choose whom to corrupt by looking at *y*. Aggregation, PAINS, solubility-limited assays and censoring-at-solubility-limit all choose by looking at *x*. This is the deepest structural mismatch.
5. **One-sided artefacts that only ever push in one direction.** Aggregation only ever makes compounds look *more* potent (S11: 95% of actives were artefacts). Solubility-limited assay ceilings only ever make compounds look *less* potent. Neither is representable as symmetric noise.
6. **Error correlated with the input in a way a model can exploit.** If error is a function of x, then a sufficiently expressive model will fit the *error* as if it were signal, and validation on data with the same x-dependent error will not reveal it. Zero-mean i.i.d. noise cannot create this failure mode; it is exactly the failure mode S12 documents in the literature.
7. **Distribution shift over time** between training and deployment (S21, S22) — orthogonal to label noise entirely.
8. **Provenance artefacts** — 90% of nominal replicate pairs being the same measurement re-cited (S1) means the *apparent* noise level in a pooled set is itself mis-estimated. No noise-injection scheme reproduces "duplicated rows that look independent".

## C) THE ONE NEW NOISE TYPE TO ADD

**Recommendation: censoring (one-sided clipping at a per-molecule threshold, then recorded as if exact).**

Implementation shape (matching what the sources describe, not invented): choose a censoring threshold τ (right-censoring: τ near the top tested concentration); for every molecule with y > τ, replace y with τ and record it as an exact value. Sweep the censored *fraction* rather than σ — 0%, 10%, 25%, 40%, 60% — because S18's effects switch on between 13% and 35%.

**Why this one:**
- **It is the most common real mechanism**, by a wide margin, with a hard number: 25–63% of labels censored in 10 of 15 real assays, **including 8% right-censoring in a LogD assay of 88,114 compounds** — LogD being one of your own endpoints (S18, Table 1, read directly from the PDF).
- **It is not zero-mean**, so it adds a genuinely new axis to your design rather than a seventh flavour of Gaussian.
- **It is what the field actually does wrong**: Landrum & Riniker (S3) explicitly discard all qualified values; typical QSAR curation keeps only relation "="; Svensson et al. (S18) show that models which ignore censoring have materially worse uncertainty estimates, with *"enhancements of the MSE ... on average, around 20% ... on the assays with > 35% censored labels."*
- **It bears directly on your uncertainty/calibration claims.** S18's whole result is that censoring is where uncertainty quantification breaks. If your paper's contribution is robustness *and* calibrated uncertainty across representations, censoring is the noise type that most sharply separates methods.
- Cheap: one threshold parameter, no per-strategy tuning, and it is trivially defensible to a reviewer with S18 as the citation.

**Runner-up, if you can afford two: a group-wise additive offset ("batch bias").** Partition the training set into K blocks by *scaffold cluster* (not by label), draw δ_k ~ N(0, σ_b) once per block, add δ_k to every label in block k. Justified by S8 (the Y = μ + R + C + ε model is the standard HTS model), S10 (≥30% of rows/columns affected in real ChemBank assays), S2 (+0.355 log method offset), and S15 (inter-lab component is 3× the intra-lab component). This is the variant that also answers (D), because the corrupted set is chosen by structure.

## D) IS THERE A DOCUMENTED MECHANISM WHERE ERROR DEPENDS ON THE MOLECULE, NOT THE LABEL?

**Yes — and this is the best-supported answer in the whole search. Your instinct that the six current strategies may be "backwards" is defensible on published evidence.**

Documented molecule-selected (structure-selected) error mechanisms:

1. **Colloidal aggregation — the strongest case.** Irwin et al. (S12) predict aggregation from **cLogP > 3 plus Tanimoto ≥ 0.85 to a known aggregator**, i.e. purely from structure and a calculated physicochemical property, and validate prospectively (5/7 at Tc 0.95–0.99, 10/19 at Tc 0.90–0.94, 3/7 at Tc 0.85–0.89). Feng et al. (S11) show the resulting error dominates the *measured signal*: *"95% of the actives can be attributed to ... aggregate-based inhibition."* Prevalence in the exact data QSAR trains on: *"61 827 or 5.1% of the ligands acting in the 0.1 to 10 μM range in the medicinal chemistry literature"* vs *"only 0.73% of all drug-like commercially available compounds"* — **the artefact is 7× enriched in the literature relative to the library it was drawn from.**
2. **Compound-specific reproducibility in the same assay type.** Cortés-Ciriano & Bender (S5): σE ranges from **0.70 (HL-60/etoposide) to 1.68 (L1210/methotrexate)**, with paclitaxel, methotrexate, zidovudine and docetaxel named as the worst-reproducing drugs. The error is indexed by compound identity, not by potency.
3. **Compound-specific interlaboratory error in a physicochemical endpoint.** Llinàs & Avdeef (S16) split 32 compounds into a *tight* set (inter-lab SD ≈ 0.17 log) and a *loose* set (SD ≈ 0.62 log). Same endpoint, same protocols, 3.6× difference in error, assigned per molecule.
4. **Substructure-defined assay interference (PAINS).** Baell & Holloway (S13) define it by substructure. **But report the caveat**: Jasial et al. (S14, secondary) find median 2–5 hits per PAINS compound across hundreds of assays and the same substructure in both consistently inactive and frequently active compounds. PAINS is a *real but weak and context-dependent* structure-selected mechanism; do not lean on it.
5. **Structure-dependent censoring.** S18's censoring thresholds are per-measurement and set by the assay's tested concentration range, which for a poorly soluble compound is bounded by its solubility. So *which* molecules get censored is partly determined by structure — a molecule-selected, one-sided, non-zero-mean error. This is the mechanism that ties (C) and (D) together.
6. **Chemotype-level systematic bias.** Krüger & Overington (S4), verbatim: *"some chemotypes have a systematic preference, or bias in binding affinity"* — bias attached to a chemical class.

**Direct implication for your design (my INFERENCE, flagged):** all six current strategies select victims by |y| or by y's quantile. The published mechanisms with the largest documented magnitudes (aggregation, compound-specific σE, censoring-at-solubility-limit) select victims by **x**. A noise strategy that picks molecules by a structural criterion — e.g. all molecules in a scaffold cluster, or all molecules above a cLogP/heavy-atom threshold — and then applies a **one-sided** shift is both better supported by the literature and a genuinely different stress test, because it is the only kind of corruption a model can learn to reproduce from its own inputs.
