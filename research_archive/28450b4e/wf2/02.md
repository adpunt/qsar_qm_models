## 1. Huber's ε-contamination model

**Primary source.** Huber PJ (1964). "Robust Estimation of a Location Parameter." *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* 35(1):73–101. DOI 10.1214/aoms/1177703732. Open access at Project Euclid.

`[READ — abstract, verbatim from Project Euclid]`
> "This paper contains a new approach toward a theory of robust estimation; it treats in detail the asymptotic theory of estimating a location parameter for contaminated normal distributions, and exhibits estimators--intermediaries between sample mean and sample median--that are asymptotically most robust"

**Caveat on the definition quote.** The line `F = (1 − ε)Φ + εH, where 0 ≤ ε < 1 is a known number, Φ(t) is the standard normal cumulative distribution, and H is an unknown contaminating distribution` was returned to me by a page-fetch summarizer reading the Project Euclid full-text page, **not by me reading the PDF** (Project Euclid blocked direct PDF download; several university mirrors also blocked). Treat it as one step removed. It is corroborated verbatim by two sources I *did* read in full:

`[READ]` van Garderen & Schluter (2025), "On (in)consistency of M-estimators under contamination", arXiv:2502.09145, §2.3, p.3:
> "The Huber ϵ-contamination model (Huber, 1964) has become the standard way of thinking about and modelling contamination. In the ϵ-contaminated normal model, the errors ε_i are drawn from a mixture F = (1 − ϵ)Φ + ϵH with ϵ ∈ [0, 1), Φ denotes the standard normal distribution and H an unknown contamination distribution. Theory often requires symmetric contamination."

`[READ]` Chen, Gao & Ren, arXiv:1511.04144v2, p.1:
> "In Huber's pathbreaking papers [10, 11] on robust estimation theory, he proposed the ǫ-contamination model (1 − ǫ)P_θ + ǫQ. Under this model, data are drawn from (1) with probability of ǫ to be contaminated by some arbitrary distribution Q."

`[READ]` Po-Ling Loh, robust-statistics short course, Fréjus 2025 (`frejus.txt`, slides), giving the neighbourhood form:
> "Definition. Consider the class of distributions with cdfs in the set P_ε(F₀) = {F : F = (1 − ε)F₀ + εH, H ∈ M}, where M is the set of all possible cdfs. This is known as (Huber's) contamination model."
> "so F also lies in the ε-neighborhood of F₀ with respect to the Kolmogorov distance."

**Parameters.** ε ∈ [0,1) contamination fraction; H the (arbitrary, often unspecified) contaminant. The base is a location family; the contaminant H is *unrestricted*, which is exactly why the model is a minimax device rather than a generative recipe.

**Standard ε values, and where they come from.** There is **no canonical fixed set of ε values** in the primary literature — I found none, and I am flagging that rather than manufacturing one. What *is* documented is the empirical gross-error rate that motivates the range:

`[READ]` Hampel F (2001), "Robust statistics: A brief introduction and overview", Seminar für Statistik ETH Zürich, Research Report No. 94, March 2001, p.1 (invited talk, Symposium "Robust Statistics and Fuzzy Techniques in Geodesy and GIS", ETH Zurich, 12–16 March 2001):
> "But real data normally contain gross errors; for scientific routine data, not taken with utmost care, their fraction is typically between 1 percent and 10 percent (!). (Nobody is perfect. In some areas, like medicine, the fraction of gross errors can easily be above 20 percent."

`[INFERENCE]` That sentence — not a theorem — is the provenance of the ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10} grid you see everywhere. The upper structural bound is the breakdown point: no translation-equivariant estimator survives ε ≥ 0.5.

`[READ]` A related, genuinely standard set of constants (van Garderen & Schluter §2.4, citing Maronna, Martin & Yohai 2006, pp. 27, 30): "Usual choices are c = 1.345 for the Huber estimator and c = 4.685 for the Tukey estimator, which give 95% efficiency relative to the average."

**Real-world mechanism.** `[READ]` Frénay & Kabán (2014, ESANN, p.667) enumerate the sources: "the available information may be insufficient to perform reliable labelling"; "even experts often make mistakes during labelling"; "classification is in some cases subjective, which results in inter-expert variability"; "incorrect labels may come from communication or encoding problems; real-word databases are estimated to contain around five percent of encoding errors". `[INFERENCE]` For your setting the ε-contamination reading is: transcription/unit errors, wrong-salt or wrong-stereoisomer assignments, assay failures — a *minority subpopulation with a different error law*, not a fattening of everyone's error.

**Variance matching.** Not possible in general: H is unrestricted and may have no variance. Variance matching only becomes well-posed once you fix H — which is §3.

## 2. Student-t noise

**Definition.** `[READ]` Bishop CM (2006), *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*, Springer, §2.3.7 "Student's t-distribution", pp. 102–104, eq. (2.159) — I read the full PDF text:
> "St(x|µ, λ, ν) = Γ(ν/2 + 1/2)/Γ(ν/2) · (λ/πν)^{1/2} · [1 + λ(x − µ)²/ν]^{−ν/2−1/2}   (2.159)"
> "The parameter λ is sometimes called the precision of the t-distribution, **even though it is not in general equal to the inverse of the variance**. The parameter ν is called the degrees of freedom... For the particular case of ν = 1, the t-distribution reduces to the Cauchy distribution, while in the limit ν → ∞ the t-distribution St(x|µ, λ, ν) becomes a Gaussian N(x|µ, λ⁻¹) with mean µ and precision λ."
> "we see that Student's t-distribution is obtained by adding up an infinite number of Gaussian distributions having the same mean but different precisions. This can be interpreted as an infinite mixture of Gaussians... The result is a distribution that in general has longer 'tails' than a Gaussian... This gives the t-distribution an important property called robustness, which means that it is much less sensitive than the Gaussian to the presence of a few data points which are outliers."

The mixture construction (2.158): `p(x|µ,a,b) = ∫₀^∞ N(x|µ,τ⁻¹) Gam(τ|a,b) dτ`, with `ν = 2a` and `λ = a/b`.

**Moments and existence.** `[READ]` Ding P. et al. / "Moments of Student's t-distribution: A Unified Approach", arXiv:1912.01607, §2:
> "The thickness of its tails is determined by the degrees of freedom. When ν = 1, the pdf in (2.2) reduces to the pdf of Cauchy(µ, σ), while the pdf in (2.2) converges to the pdf of the normal N(t|µ, (1/√σ)²) as ν → ∞. While the tails of the normal distribution decay at an exponential rate, the Student's t-distribution is heavy-tailed, with a polynomial decay rate."
> "In general, the moments are undefined when k ≥ ν."

⚠️ **Parameterisation trap.** That paper's σ is a *precision-like* parameter: it states `E(T) = µ, Var(T) = (1/σ)·ν/(ν−2)` for ν > 2. Bishop's λ is likewise a precision, not 1/variance. The conventional *scale* parameterisation is different. Verified independently:

`[READ]` Probability Distribution Explorer (distribution-explorer.github.io), "Student-t distribution": PDF `f(y;ν,μ,σ) = Γ((ν+1)/2)/(Γ(ν/2)√(πνσ²)) · (1 + (y−μ)²/(νσ²))^{−(ν+1)/2}`; **Mean** "μ for ν > 1, otherwise undefined"; **Variance** "νσ²/(ν−2)" for ν > 2, **infinite** for 1 < ν < 2, **undefined** for ν ≤ 1.

So in the scale parameterisation (μ, s, ν): **Var = s²·ν/(ν−2), ν > 2.** Use this one; state it explicitly in your methods, because three conventions are in circulation.

**Robust-modelling citation.** `[READ]` Lange K, Little RJA, Taylor JMG (1989), "Robust Statistical Modeling Using the t Distribution", *JASA* 84(408):881–896 (Dec 1989), JSTOR 2290063. Abstract verbatim (from the eScholarship copy, item qt27s1d3h7):
> "The t distribution provides a useful extension of the normal for statistical modeling of data sets involving errors with longer-than-normal tails. An analytical strategy based on maximum likelihood for a general model with multivariate t errors is suggested and applied to a variety of problems, including linear and nonlinear regression, robust estimation of the mean and covariance matrix with missing data, unbalanced multivariate repeated-measures data, multivariate modeling of pedigree data, and multivariate nonlinear regression. The degrees of freedom parameter of the t distribution provides a convenient dimension for achieving robust statistical inference, with moderate increases in computational complexity for many models."

⚠️ `[READ, degraded]` The body of that PDF extracts with a **+29 byte shift and column scrambling**. I recovered a fragment reading "…that the value v = 4 has … sensible value we have found … worked well in many … data set…". **I will not present this as a verbatim quote.** The *sense* — that ν = 4 is a sensible default the authors found to work well across many data sets — is what the fragment says, but if you cite ν = 4 to Lange et al. you must confirm it against a clean copy of the paper first.

**Real-world mechanism.** `[INFERENCE]` t-noise says *every* measurement is drawn from the same law, but the per-measurement precision itself varies (Bishop's Gamma-mixture reading). That is a good model for an assay where run-to-run precision fluctuates — no distinguished "bad subpopulation", just occasional bad days.

## 3. Mixture of Gaussians / Tukey's contaminated normal

**Primary source.** Tukey JW (1960), "A survey of sampling from contaminated distributions", in Olkin I (ed.), *Contributions to Probability and Statistics*, Stanford University Press. ⚠️ **I could not obtain this chapter** — it is a book chapter, not online. Everything below is **secondary attribution**, and I flag it as such.

`[READ]` Gerstenberger C & Vogel D (2014), "On the efficiency of Gini's mean difference", arXiv:1405.5027, §2.3, p.6 — read in full:
> "**2.3. The normal mixture distribution.** The normal mixture distribution NM(λ, ε), sometimes also referred to as contaminated normal distribution, is defined as
> NM(λ, ε) = (1 − ε)N(0, 1) + εN(0, λ²),  0 ≤ ε ≤ 1, λ ≥ 1.
> ... It captures the notion that the majority of the data stems from the normal distribution, except for some small fraction ε which stems from another, usually heavier-tailed, contamination distribution. In case of the normal mixture model, this contamination distribution is the Gaussian distribution with standard deviation λ. **This type of contamination model has been popularized by Tukey (1960), who also argues that λ = 3 is a sensible choice in practice.**"

And their abstract, on Tukey's headline result:
> "In his seminal 1960 paper A survey of sampling from contaminated distributions, J. W. Tukey points out that, if the normal distribution is contaminated by a small ε-fraction of a normal distribution with three times the standard deviation, the mean deviation is more efficient than the standard deviation—already for ε < 1%."

And on ε (p.13):
> "Fixing λ = 3, we find that for ε = 0.00175, the mean deviation is as efficient as the standard deviation. **Tukey (1960) gives a value of ε = 0.008.**"

**Standard parameterisation:** `(1−ε)·N(0, σ₀²) + ε·N(0, λ²σ₀²)`, λ = 3 conventional (Tukey, via Gerstenberger & Vogel). **Typical contamination fractions:** the values with actual documented provenance are Tukey's ε ≈ 0.008 crossover and Hampel's empirical 1–10% band (§1). ε = 0.01/0.05/0.10 is the de facto grid; `[INFERENCE]` that grid is convention, not a cited constant.

**Real-world mechanism.** `[INFERENCE]` This is the explicit generative version of §1: a named minority of records genuinely produced by a worse process (a bad plate, a mis-keyed unit, a compound measured at the assay's dynamic-range limit).

## 4. Laplace / double exponential

**Definition.** `[READ]` "Statistical Analysis for Small and Big Data" §37 (quarto.wessa.net/laplace.html): PDF `f(x) = (1/2b) exp(−|x−μ|/b)`, μ ∈ ℝ location, b > 0 scale; **E(X) = μ**; **V(X) = 2b²**; excess kurtosis 3.

`[COMPUTED — verified]` `scipy.stats.laplace.stats(moments='k')` returns 3.0. Independent confirmation of the excess-kurtosis value.

**Median-regression link.** `[READ]` Geraci M & Borja MC (2018), "Notebook: The Laplace distribution", *Significance* 15:10–11, DOI 10.1111/j.1740-9713.2018.01185.x:
> "the MLE of the location parameter is the sample median, while an estimate of the scale parameter is obtained through the absolute value of the residuals."

(Note: that article also says "the scale parameter σ to its standard deviation" — with the (1/2b)exp(−|x−μ|/b) density that is **wrong**, SD = √2·b. Use the wessa/standard variance, not that phrase.)

`[READ]` Koenker R & Hallock KF (2001), "Quantile Regression", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 15(4):143–156 (read from econ.uiuc.edu/~roger/research/rq/QRJEP.pdf):
> "Just as we can define the sample mean as the solution to the problem of minimizing a sum of squared residuals, we can define the median as the solution to the problem of minimizing a sum of absolute residuals."
> "To obtain an estimate of the conditional median function, we simply replace the scalar ξ in the first equation by the parametric function ξ(x_i, β) and set τ to ½. Variants of this idea were proposed in the mid-eighteenth century by Boscovich and subsequently investigated by Laplace and Edgeworth, among others."

Canonical regression citation: Koenker R & Bassett G (1978), "Regression Quantiles", *Econometrica* 46(1):33–50. `[READ — bibliographic record only]`; I could not download the PDF (Berkeley mirror returned a PHP error page). The median-regression-as-special-case claim is sourced above to Koenker & Hallock 2001 instead.

**Real-world mechanism.** `[INFERENCE]` Laplace is only mildly heavy-tailed (excess kurtosis 3, versus ∞ for t with ν ≤ 4). It is best justified as "the noise law under which your model's loss is a *median* regression", i.e. it is a *loss* statement more than an *error-mechanism* statement. It is a poor model of gross errors.

## 5. Label-noise taxonomy in machine learning

**Primary taxonomy.** Frénay B & Verleysen M (2014), "Classification in the Presence of Label Noise: A Survey", *IEEE Trans. Neural Netw. Learn. Syst.* 25(5):845–869, DOI 10.1109/TNNLS.2013.2292894, PMID 24808033. ⚠️ **IEEE paywalled — I did not read it.** I read the authors' own open-access companion, which states the taxonomy explicitly and attributes it to that paper:

`[READ]` Frénay B & Kabán A (2014), "A Comprehensive Introduction to Label Noise", *ESANN 2014 Proceedings*, Bruges, 23–25 April 2014, pp. 667–676, ISBN 978-287419095-7 (esann.org/sites/default/files/proceedings/legacy/es2014-10.pdf), p.667:
> "Figure 1 shows a taxonomy of label noise, proposed in [1] and inspired by the work of Schafer and Graham [15]. Three types of noise are distinguished here. **First, label noise completely at random (NCAR) occurs independently of the true class and of the values of the instance features. Second, label noise that occurs at random (NAR) depends only on the true label.** This can be used to model situations where some classes are more likely to be mislabelled than others. **Third, label noise not at random (NNAR) is the more general case, where the mislabelling probability also depends on the feature values.** This allows us to model labelling errors near the classification boundaries."

Here `[1]` is Frénay & Verleysen TNNLS 2014 and `[15]` is Schafer JL & Graham JW (2002), "Missing data: our view of the state of the art", *Psychological Methods* 7(2):147–177 — i.e. **the taxonomy is a deliberate transplant of Rubin's missing-data mechanism hierarchy** (Rubin DB, 1976, "Inference and missing data", *Biometrika* 63(3):581–592; `[READ — bibliographic record confirmed via Oxford Academic listing and Harvard DASH]`, paper itself not opened).

`[READ]` Also from the same page: "Most studies only deal with NCAR or NAR label noise, but NNAR label noise is also studied e.g. in [26]." And p.669: "In most experiments, label noise is artificially introduced in datasets. **NCAR label noise is introduced by picking instances at random**... strategies to consider NAR label noise... a few works deal with NNAR label noise which is introduced in ambiguous regions."

**Song et al. 2022/2023 — the paper your draft already cites.** Song H, Kim M, Park D, Shin Y, Lee J-G, "Learning From Noisy Labels With Deep Neural Networks: A Survey", *IEEE TNNLS* 34(11):8135–8153 (2023; first published 2022), DOI 10.1109/TNNLS.2022.3152527, arXiv:2007.08199. `[READ — arXiv full text]`, §II-B:
> "A detailed analysis of the taxonomy of label noise was provided by Frénay and Verleysen [13]. Most existing algorithms dealt with instance-independent noise, but instance-dependent noise has not yet been extensively investigated owing to its complex modeling."
> "**1) Instance-independent Label Noise:** ... the true label is corrupted by a noise transition matrix T ∈ [0,1]^{c×c}, where T_ij := p(ỹ = j|y = i)... the noise is called a **symmetric (or uniform) noise with a noise rate τ ∈ [0,1]** if ∀_{i=j} T_ij = 1 − τ ∧ ∀_{i≠j} T_ij = τ/(c−1)... the noise is called an **asymmetric (or label-dependent) noise** if..."
> "**2) Instance-dependent Label Noise:** ... the corruption probability is defined as ρ_ij(x) = p(ỹ = j|y = i, x)."

### Mapping onto regression — **is there an accepted equivalent?**

**Short answer: no full one.** The only thing Song et al. commit to is a two-way split, given in §II-D verbatim `[READ]`:

> "**D. Regression with Noisy Labels.** ... Given the input feature x and its ground-truth label y, two types of label noise are considered in the regression task. **An additive noise [65] is formulated by ỹ := y + ε where ε is drawn from a random distribution independent from the input feature; an instance-dependent noise [66] is formulated by ỹ := ρ(x) where ρ : X → Y is a noise function dependent on the input feature.** Although regression predicts continuous values, regression and classification share the same concept of learning the mapping function from the input feature x to the output label y. Thus, many robust approaches for classification are easily extended to the regression problem with simple modification [67]. Thus, **in this survey, we focus on the classification setting** for which most robust methods are defined."

`[INFERENCE]` The mapping is therefore:
- **NCAR → additive, x- and y-independent noise** (ỹ = y + ε, ε ⊥ x, ε ⊥ y). This is the only branch with a clean, universally accepted regression analogue, and it is exactly Song et al.'s "additive noise".
- **NAR → label-dependent noise**: ε whose distribution depends on y (heteroscedasticity in the target — e.g. larger error at high pIC50). ⚠️ **There is no standard name for this in the regression label-noise literature.** Song et al. do not have this category; they collapse anything x-dependent into "instance-dependent".
- **NNAR → instance-dependent noise**, ỹ := ρ(x) (Song et al.'s second type).

`[INFERENCE]` So if you use NCAR/NAR/NNAR language for regression you are **extending** the taxonomy, not applying an established one, and you should say so in one sentence. The defensible framing: "we adopt the label-noise taxonomy of Frénay & Verleysen (2014) — NCAR/NAR/NNAR — in its regression form, where Song et al. (2023) distinguish only *additive* (feature-independent) from *instance-dependent* noise; our σ-independent strategies are NCAR/additive, our value-proportional and heteroscedastic strategies are the y- and x-dependent extensions."

`[READ — search-result level only, NOT VERIFIED by reading]` Multiple 2023–2025 sources (e.g. a *Expert Systems with Applications* 2023 label-noise-filtering-for-regression paper) state that regression label-noise handling is less developed because "the target space is continuous and lacks discrete output logits". I did not open these; do not cite them without checking.

## A) One heavy-tailed model that is standard, defensible, and easy to variance-match

**Student-t with degrees of freedom ν, in the location–scale parameterisation, restricted to ν > 2.**

Reasons, each grounded:
1. **One knob, monotone in tail weight, with the Gaussian as an exact endpoint.** `[READ, Bishop 2006 §2.3.7]` "in the limit ν → ∞ the t-distribution St(x|µ,λ,ν) becomes a Gaussian". So your existing Gaussian arm is the ν → ∞ limit of the new arm — the experiment nests, and the tail axis is a single scalar.
2. **Closed-form variance, so variance matching is one line of algebra** (§B).
3. **It is the canonical robust noise/likelihood model with a top-tier citation** — Lange, Little & Taylor 1989 JASA `[READ, abstract]`: "The degrees of freedom parameter of the t distribution provides a convenient dimension for achieving robust statistical inference."
4. **It is a Gaussian scale mixture** `[READ, Bishop eq. 2.158]`, so it is interpretable as per-molecule varying precision — which is directly the story your uncertainty-quantification models are supposed to detect.

**Why not the contaminated normal as the single choice:** `[INFERENCE]` it has *two* parameters (ε, λ) that trade off against each other, so "amount of tail" is not one-dimensional and results are harder to plot on a single axis. It is the better *second* arm — precisely because it is Huber's/Tukey's model and a reviewer will look for it. Recommended pairing: **t (ν) as the primary tail axis, contaminated normal (ε=0.05, λ=3) as one anchor point tying you to Huber 1964 / Tukey 1960.**

**Do not use Laplace as your heavy-tail arm.** `[COMPUTED]` Its excess kurtosis is fixed at 3 — lighter than t with ν ≤ 6 — so it gives you one point, not an axis. It belongs in the paper only if you also fit an L1/median-regression model.

## B) Holding "amount of noise" constant while varying tail heaviness

All algebra below is `[COMPUTED]` by me; every numeric column was verified with `scipy.stats` (an independent implementation) and the contaminated-normal kurtosis was additionally confirmed by 2×10⁷-sample Monte Carlo (simulated variance 1.0002, simulated excess kurtosis 4.636 vs algebraic 4.653 for ε=0.05, λ=3).

### Student-t

Draw `ỹ = y + s·T_ν`, T_ν standard t. Then `Var = s²·ν/(ν−2)` for ν > 2 `[READ, distribution-explorer]`.

**To achieve a target noise SD of σ:**

$$s = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}}$$

(`6/(ν−4)` checked against `scipy.stats.t.stats(ν, moments='k')` — exact agreement at ν = 5, 6, 7, 10, 30.)

**🚩 Undefined/infinite cases — flag these explicitly:**
- **ν ≤ 2: variance is infinite (1 < ν < 2) or undefined (ν ≤ 1)** `[READ, distribution-explorer]`. **You cannot variance-match at ν ≤ 2.** ν = 1 is Cauchy `[READ, Bishop]` — even the mean does not exist.
- **2 < ν ≤ 4: variance exists but kurtosis does not** `[READ, arXiv:1912.01607: "the moments are undefined when k ≥ ν"]`. Variance matching still works; any *kurtosis*-matched comparison does not.
- `[INFERENCE]` Practical grid: **ν ∈ {3, 5, 10, 30, ∞}**. ν = 3 gives a genuinely infinite-kurtosis arm while keeping variance finite — a strong, honest demonstration. Avoid ν ≤ 2 entirely unless you deliberately want the "no finite variance" pathology, in which case you must switch invariant (see C).
- **Sampling caveat** `[INFERENCE]`: with finite n, a variance-matched t₃ sample has a *sample* variance that is wildly unstable (its own variance depends on the 4th moment, which is infinite). If you report empirical noise SD per run, expect it to fluctuate hard at low ν. Fix the *population* σ, and say so.

### Contaminated normal

Draw `ỹ = y + e`, `e ~ (1−ε)·N(0, σ₀²) + ε·N(0, λ²σ₀²)`.

$$\mathrm{Var}(e) = (1-\varepsilon)\sigma_0^2 + \varepsilon\lambda^2\sigma_0^2 = \sigma_0^2\left[1 + \varepsilon(\lambda^2-1)\right]$$

**To achieve target SD σ:**

$$\boxed{\ \sigma_0 = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon(\lambda^2 - 1)}}\ }, \qquad \text{contaminant SD} = \lambda\sigma_0$$

Excess kurtosis (both components zero-mean):
$$\kappa_{\text{ex}} = \frac{3\left[(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\lambda^4\right]}{\left[(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\lambda^2\right]^2} - 3$$

| ε | λ | σ₀/σ | contaminant SD /σ | excess kurtosis | equivalent t ν |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 3 | 0.9623 | 2.887 | 1.63 | 7.68 |
| 0.05 | 3 | 0.8452 | 2.536 | 4.65 | 5.29 |
| 0.10 | 3 | 0.7454 | 2.236 | 5.33 | 5.13 |
| 0.20 | 3 | 0.6202 | 1.861 | 4.54 | 5.32 |
| 0.05 | 5 | 0.6742 | 3.371 | 16.96 | 4.35 |
| 0.05 | 10 | 0.4100 | 4.100 | 39.45 | 4.15 |
| 0.10 | 10 | 0.3029 | 3.029 | 22.27 | 4.27 |

("equivalent t ν" = solve 6/(ν−4) = κ_ex; valid only for ν > 4.)

**Variance is always finite here** — every component is Gaussian, so *no* (ε, λ) combination breaks variance matching. `[INFERENCE]` That is the contaminated normal's main practical advantage over t, and the reason to keep it in the design as the "safe" heavy-tail arm.

**Note the non-monotonicity** `[COMPUTED]`: at λ = 3, kurtosis *peaks* near ε ≈ 0.10 and falls again by ε = 0.20 — because at large ε the mixture becomes just a wider Gaussian. Do not read ε as a monotone "tail heaviness" dial.

### Laplace

`Var = 2b²` `[READ]` ⟹ **b = σ/√2 = 0.7071σ**. Excess kurtosis fixed at 3, matching t with ν = 6 exactly `[COMPUTED]`.

## C) Is variance the right invariant?

**Short answer: variance is the right *primary* invariant for your paper, but it is not the only defensible one, and it silently changes the central behaviour of the noise.** Here is the honest picture.

### The problem with variance

`[COMPUTED]` Look at the last column of the t table. Variance-matched t₃ has a **median absolute deviation of 0.44σ vs the Gaussian's 0.67σ** — a third smaller. So "equal variance" means the *typical* molecule gets **less** perturbation under t₃ than under the Gaussian, and the variance is repaid by a handful of huge outliers. Same for the contaminated normal: at ε=0.05, λ=3, the 95% of clean molecules get 0.845σ, not σ.

`[INFERENCE]` This is not a bug — it is precisely what "heavy-tailed at matched total energy" means, and it is arguably exactly the effect you want to isolate. But it must be stated in the paper, or a reviewer will read "same σ" as "same perturbation per molecule" and the whole comparison will be misread.

### Alternatives that appear in the literature

1. **MAD (median absolute deviation)** and **IQR** — the two standard robust scale measures. `[READ, van Garderen & Schluter §2.4, citing Maronna et al. 2006 p.36]`: "Tuning parameters that ensure consistency under normality are k = 1/(q^Φ_{3/4} − q^Φ_{1/4}) for IQR and k = 1/q^Φ_{1/4} for MAD, where q^Φ_p is the standard normal p-quantile." Both are **defined for every distribution including Cauchy**, so they let you compare ν ≤ 2 arms that variance cannot reach. `[READ, same paper, abstract]` — caveat: "robust scale estimators such as the IQR and MAD are inconsistent under contamination", so they are not neutral either.
2. **Mean deviation / Gini's mean difference** — `[READ, Gerstenberger & Vogel 2014]` the whole point of Tukey (1960) was that under contamination the *ranking* of scale measures reverses. This is direct evidence that **the choice of scale invariant is itself a substantive modelling decision, not a normalisation detail.**
3. **Noise rate ε or τ.** In the *label-noise* literature specifically, **this is the standard invariant — not variance.** `[READ, Song et al. 2023 §II-B]`: "the noise is called a symmetric (or uniform) noise with a **noise rate τ ∈ [0,1]**". Every headline number in that field ("40% symmetric noise", Fig. 5 of Song et al.) is a *fraction of corrupted labels*, not an error magnitude. `[READ, Frénay & Kabán 2014 p.669]`: "NCAR label noise is introduced by picking instances at random". `[INFERENCE]` That convention is a classification artefact — a flipped discrete label has no magnitude — and **it does not transfer to regression**, where magnitude is the whole story.
4. **Scaling to the label range — the actual QSAR convention.** `[READ]` Kolmar SS & Grulke CM (2021), "The effect of noise on the predictive limit of QSAR models", *J. Cheminform.* 13:92, DOI 10.1186/s13321-021-00571-7 (read via PMC8613965): noise was "Gaussian distributed with zero mean", with `σ_noise,n = (Y_max − Y_min) × multiplier × n`, multiplier = 0.01, n = 0…14 → 15 levels, 5 replicates each. `[READ — search-snippet level, NOT read in full]` Cortés-Ciriano I & Bender A (2015), *JCIM* 55(7):1413–1425, DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.5b00101, reportedly sampled "from Gaussian distributions with increasingly larger variances, which ranged from zero to the range of pIC50 values" — **I did not open this paper; verify before citing that sentence.**

### Recommendation

`[INFERENCE]`
- **Match variance as the primary invariant.** It is the only invariant under which "total noise energy" is conserved, it is what makes R²/RMSE degradation comparable across arms, it is trivially computable for t (ν>2), contaminated normal, and Laplace, and it is what the QSAR noise-injection precedents (Kolmar & Grulke 2021) effectively fix.
- **Report MAD alongside σ for every arm** — one extra column, using the numbers in the table above. It pre-empts the "but the typical molecule sees less noise" objection and makes the mechanism visible rather than hidden.
- **Do not use noise-rate as the invariant** despite its dominance in the label-noise literature; it is a classification convention (Song et al. 2023 explicitly restrict their survey to classification for this reason) and would make your contaminated-normal arm incomparable to your Gaussian arm.
- **If you want a ν ≤ 2 / Cauchy arm**, you must switch to MAD- or IQR-matching for that arm and say so explicitly — variance matching is *mathematically undefined* there, not merely awkward.
