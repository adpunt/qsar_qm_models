# Noise-injection design, rebuilt from the published evidence

Prepared from the five verified research reports plus my own reading of `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/rust/src/main.rs` and `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/data/QM9/raw/gdb9.sdf.csv`.

Every statement carries a tag. **[READ]** = quoted from a source someone actually opened. **[COMPUTED]** = I calculated it in this session; the command is reproducible. **[INFERENCE]** = my judgement. **[NOT VERIFIED]** = could not be confirmed; do not cite.

Two terms used throughout, defined once:

- **Label error** means the difference between the number recorded in a database and the true value of the property.
- **Dose** means the root-mean-square size of the perturbation actually added to the labels. It is defined precisely in Part 4. It is the thing that must be held constant.

## PART 1 — WHAT REAL EXPERIMENTAL ERROR LOOKS LIKE

### 1.1 How big it is

For potency measurements pooled from many laboratories, the standard deviation of a single measurement is a little over half a log unit.

- pIC50: standard deviation 0.68, mean unsigned error 0.55, median unsigned error 0.43 log units, from 10,895 values covering 3,480 protein–ligand systems in ChEMBL 14. **[READ, Kalliokoski et al. 2013, PLoS ONE 8(4):e61007, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0061007]**
- pKi: standard deviation 0.54, mean error 0.44, median error 0.34 log units. **[READ, Kramer et al. 2012, J Med Chem 55:5165–5173, DOI 10.1021/jm300131x — abstract only, full text paywalled]**
- Cytotoxicity pIC50: standard deviation 0.76–1.00 log units. **[READ, Cortés-Ciriano & Bender 2016, ChemMedChem 11:57–71, DOI 10.1002/cmdc.201500424 — abstract only]**

Within one laboratory running one assay, the error is three to four times smaller: standard deviation 0.22 for rolipram against PDE4D and 0.17 for cilostamide against PDE3. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013]**

That gap between within-laboratory and between-laboratory error is the single most important structural fact in the whole evidence base. It recurs everywhere. For intrinsic solubility, Avdeef reports that "the intralaboratory errors reported in the CheqSol method (0.05 log) need to be multiplied by a factor of 3 to match the expected interlaboratory errors for the method." **[READ, Avdeef 2019, ADMET & DMPK 7(3):210–219, DOI 10.5599/admet.698]**

### 1.2 It is not Gaussian — the tails are much fatter

Three independent lines of evidence, in descending order of directness.

**One formal normality test exists, and it rejects normality.** Krüger and Overington tested the distribution of bioactivity differences and found it "is non-normal as established by Anderson-Darling test (p<2e-16)", then fitted a Laplace distribution with scale 0.7 for paralogues and 1.3 for human-to-rat orthologues. **[READ, Krüger & Overington 2012, PLoS Comput Biol 8(1):e1002333, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002333]** Important caveat: that test and that fit were applied to biological difference distributions, not to the pure between-assay error distribution. The paper says the between-assay distribution "closely resembles the distribution for orthologs" but never fits it directly. **[READ]** So this is strong evidence, not conclusive evidence.

**A fitted Gaussian width grows as you include more of the tail.** Kalliokoski fitted a Gaussian only to the truncated core of the difference distribution, cutting at 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 log units. The fitted standard deviation rose from 0.80 to 0.84 to 0.86 for pIC50, and from 0.66 to 0.68 to 0.68 for pKi. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013, Table 3]** If the distribution really were Gaussian the fitted width would not depend on where you cut. **[INFERENCE]**

**The far tail is astronomically improbable under a Gaussian.** The largest observed difference was 7.7 log units among 16,844 pairs. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013, Figure 2 caption]** With the fitted width of 0.86 that is 8.95 standard deviations. A Gaussian predicts 5.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ such pairs in 16,844; a Laplace predicts 0.05; a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom predicts 3.8. **[COMPUTED]**

**A 2024 replication gives clean printed numbers.** For IC50 assay pairs in ChEMBL 32 under minimal curation, "64% of the Δpchembl values are greater than 0.3, and 27% are greater than 1.0". **[READ, Landrum & Riniker 2024, J Chem Inf Model 64(5):1560–1567, DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.4c00049, verified against the local full text in the scratchpad]** A Gaussian tuned so that 64% of pairs exceed 0.3 log units predicts only 11.9% exceeding 1.0 log unit. The observed 27% is **2.3 times** what a Gaussian allows. **[COMPUTED]**

### 1.3 How heavy the tail actually is — and why this matters more than it looks

I fitted symmetric distributions to the two printed Landrum fractions. Each family was scaled so that exactly 64% of pairs exceed 0.3 log units, then asked what fraction exceeds 1.0 log unit. **[all COMPUTED]**

| Family | Predicted fraction beyond 1.0 log unit | Observed 0.27 |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | 0.119 | 2.3× too small |
| Student-t, 10 degrees of freedom | 0.139 | 1.9× too small |
| Student-t, 5 degrees of freedom | 0.158 | 1.7× too small |
| Student-t, 3 degrees of freedom | 0.182 | 1.5× too small |
| Laplace | 0.226 | 1.2× too small |
| Student-t, 1.1 degrees of freedom | 0.270 | matches |

The finding is blunt: **no standard heavy-tailed family with a finite variance reproduces the real tail.** Student-t only reaches the observed tail at about 1.1 degrees of freedom, which has neither a variance nor a mean. **[COMPUTED]** I also tried two-component Gaussian mixtures across contamination fractions 0.05 to 0.30 and width ratios 2 to 4; none reached 0.27 either. **[COMPUTED]**

This has a direct design consequence. Heavy-tailed noise is necessary but a single heavy-tailed distribution is not sufficient. Something discrete and large is also going on. Part 1.4 says what.

**Two honest caveats on this section.** First, the ratio test assumes the fraction beyond 0.3 and the fraction beyond 1.0 come from the same underlying distribution of one measurement pair; that is what the paper reports, but the paper's Methods list defines R², Kendall's tau, the two fractions, Cohen's kappa and Matthews correlation — **it never defines what its "MAE" column means**, so I have deliberately not used that column. **[READ, Landrum & Riniker 2024, "Quantifying Assay Compatibility" section]** Second, the widely repeated moment-ratio argument that the *core* of the error distribution is mildly heavy-tailed with about 10 degrees of freedom does not survive checking. Kalliokoski's triple (standard deviation 0.68, mean unsigned error 0.55, median unsigned error 0.43) gives a standard-deviation-to-median ratio of 1.581, above the Gaussian value of 1.483, but a standard-deviation-to-mean ratio of 1.236, **below** the Gaussian value of 1.253. No symmetric distribution can be simultaneously heavier and lighter than a Gaussian. **[COMPUTED]** The triple is internally inconsistent, almost certainly because the standard deviation came from a truncated fit while the other two did not. Do not build anything on that ratio.

### 1.4 The tail is mostly outright mistakes, not imprecision

Kalliokoski manually inspected ten pairs at each level of disagreement. Of pairs differing by 4.7–7.8 log units, 9 out of 10 were invalid. At 3.2 log units, 10 out of 10. At 2.5, 8 out of 10. Even at 1.5 log units, 6 out of 10 were invalid. The error types were unit-transcription mistakes, wrong receptor subtype, cell-based results confused with biochemical ones, undifferentiated stereochemistry, and retracted papers. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013, Table 2]** The figure caption for the pairwise plot states plainly: "The extreme disagreements are all due to clear errors." **[READ]**

Landrum and Riniker found a concrete instance: 32 carbonic-anhydrase-I assays sharing a corresponding author with internally inconsistent results. They wrote that "because this is almost certainly artificial variability and not just experimental noise, we removed all data from assays that have more than ten compounds in common." **[READ]**

Kramer's filtering note is the sharpest single statement of how corrupted the raw data is: ChEMBL "contains unit-transcription errors, undifferentiated stereoisomers, and repeated citations of single measurements (90% of all pairs)." **[READ, abstract]**

**So the correct picture is two-part: a moderate, roughly log-normal measurement error, plus a minority of records that are simply wrong by a large amount.** **[INFERENCE, but directly supported by the three sources above.]**

### 1.5 Error does NOT depend on the value of the label

This is the most important negative result in the whole review, and it contradicts four of the six strategies currently implemented.

Kalliokoski tested this directly and rejected it. Verbatim:

> "We checked whether the ΔpIC50 depends on the overall activity measured or on physicochemical ligand properties like logP, logD, molecular weight (MW), polar surface area (PSA)... **The ΔpIC50's depend neither on the average measured pIC50 nor on any of the ligand properties examined.**" **[READ]**

And on the specific hypothesis that weak compounds are noisier because of solubility limits:

> "One might assume that higher IC50 values show a larger variability than for example single digit µM IC50 values because of solubility limits. **However, our analysis shows that on the average this is clearly not the case.**" **[READ]**

Industry practice encodes the opposite of value-dependence. The Assay Guidance Manual's precision statistic, the Minimum Significant Ratio, is a constant fold-ratio computed from the standard deviation of the **logarithm** of potency, with a quality-control expectation that it be "consistent over a range of potencies." **[READ, Assay Guidance Manual, "Assay Operations for SAR Support", NCBI Bookshelf NBK91994]** AstraZeneca's company-wide reproducibility analysis of all biological assays from 2005 to 2014 reported "an average experimental uncertainty of less than a twofold difference and no technologies or assay types had higher variability than others" — a ratio, which means a constant spread in log space. **[READ, Kramer et al. 2016, Drug Discov Today 21(8):1213–1221, DOI 10.1016/j.drudis.2016.03.015 — abstract only]**

There is one genuine value-dependence law in analytical chemistry, and it points the same way. The Horwitz equation states that relative standard deviation "doubled for every decrease of 2 orders of magnitude in concentration", equivalently absolute standard deviation ∝ concentration^0.85. **[READ, Horwitz & Albert 2006, J AOAC Int 89(4):1095ff]** That is approximately constant *relative* error on the raw scale, which becomes approximately constant *absolute* error once you take logarithms. Since pIC50, logD, log solubility and log permeability are already logarithms, the transform has already absorbed it. **[INFERENCE]**

I could find **no primary source at all** reporting a measured relationship between logD error and the logD value, or between Caco-2 permeability error and the permeability value. **[NOT VERIFIED — absent, not contradicted.]**

### 1.6 Error DOES depend on the molecule and on where the data came from

This is where the real structure lives.

**Colloidal aggregation** is measurement error selected purely by chemical structure. Irwin and colleagues predict it from calculated logP above 3 plus a Tanimoto similarity of 0.85 or more to a known aggregator, and validated prospectively: 5 of 7 molecules at similarity 0.95–0.99 aggregated, 10 of 19 at 0.90–0.94, 3 of 7 at 0.85–0.89. **[READ, Irwin et al. 2015, J Med Chem 58(17):7076–7087, DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01105]** They also report that "61 827 or 5.1% of the ligands acting in the 0.1 to 10 μM range in the medicinal chemistry literature are at least 85% similar to a known aggregator", versus "only 0.73% of all drug-like commercially available compounds" — the artefact is enriched sevenfold in exactly the literature QSAR trains on. **[READ]** Feng and colleagues, screening 70,563 molecules, found that "95% of the actives can be attributed to a single one of these: aggregate-based inhibition." **[READ, Feng et al. 2007, J Med Chem 50(10):2385–2390, DOI 10.1021/jm061317y]**

**Error size varies between molecules within one endpoint.** Cortés-Ciriano and Bender report the estimated error ranging from 0.70 for the HL-60/etoposide system to 1.68 for L1210/methotrexate — a 2.4-fold spread, indexed by compound identity, not by potency. **[READ, abstract]** Llinàs and Avdeef split 32 solubility test compounds into a tight set with between-laboratory standard deviation about 0.17 log and a loose set with about 0.62 log — a 3.6-fold difference, assigned per molecule. **[READ, title of Llinàs & Avdeef 2019, J Chem Inf Model 59(6):3036–3040; body text NOT VERIFIED]**

**High-throughput screening error is shared by blocks of compounds.** The standard model in the field is written as a plate-specific row effect plus a plate-specific column effect plus a residual, explicitly in place of the naive independent-normal model. **[READ, Malo et al. 2006, Nat Biotechnol 24(2):167–175, DOI 10.1038/nbt1186]** On a real 1,250-plate assay, correcting for the positional bias changed the hit list from 96 hits to 26. **[READ, Dragiev et al. 2011, BMC Bioinformatics 12:25, DOI 10.1186/1471-2105-12-25, Table 5]**

### 1.7 A large fraction of real labels are censored, not noisy

Censoring means the assay reports "greater than X" or "less than X" instead of a number, because the true value lies outside the tested range. In a real industrial dataset of 15 assays, the censored fraction ranged from 0% to 63%, with CYP2C9 at 63%, CYP3A4 at 61%, hERG at 42%, and — directly relevant here — **logD at 8% right-censored across 88,114 compounds**. In the same assays the replicate standard deviation from control compounds was only 0.10 to 0.36 log units. **[READ, Svensson et al. 2025, Artificial Intelligence in the Life Sciences 7:100128, DOI 10.1016/j.ailsci.2025.100128, Table 1]** The censored fraction is a far larger effect than the replicate spread.

Censored labels are the one documented error mechanism that is **one-sided**. Every noise strategy currently in the codebase is symmetric and averages to zero. **[INFERENCE]**

### 1.8 Where the evidence is thin — stated plainly

- **logD.** There is no primary published standard deviation I could verify. The figure "0.27" recorded in project memory could not be traced to any source and should be deleted. **[NOT VERIFIED]** The best available estimate, 0.43–0.62 log units, was **[COMPUTED]** by dividing a deliberately worst-case max-minus-min repeat statistic (mean absolute difference 0.48, root-mean-square difference 0.881) by √2, from Niu et al. 2024, Sci Data 11:985, DOI 10.1038/s41597-024-03793-0, Table 5 **[Table READ]**. It is an upper bound, not a printed number.
- **Caco-2 permeability.** No verified number. The best candidate is a between-dataset root-mean-square error of 0.581 across six literature sets and ten compounds, from Lee et al. 2017, Eur J Pharm Biopharm 114:38–42, DOI 10.1016/j.ejpb.2016.12.027 **[abstract READ]**. **The abstract does not state that 0.581 is in log units** and the full text was unreachable. If it is log units, a single-laboratory standard deviation of about 0.41 follows. **[COMPUTED, conditional]** The "0.43 via a 20.9-fold atenolol range in Hayeshi 2008" chain in project memory is broken: the Hayeshi abstract confirms the ten-laboratory design but contains no such figure. **[NOT VERIFIED]**
- **hERG.** Project memory records "hERG pKi 0.54". That number is Kramer's general figure for all heterogeneous public Ki data across all targets, not an hERG number. **[READ — scope misattributed.]** A genuinely hERG-specific figure now exists: residual standard deviation 0.18 log units across five laboratories and 28 drugs under a standardised best-practice protocol. **[READ, Alvarez Baron et al. 2025, Sci Rep 15, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-15761-8]**
- **QM9.** There is no experimental error to model. Kolmar and Grulke state it directly: "quantum mechanical calculations do not have random experimental error, because the same calculation will give exactly the same number." **[READ, Kolmar & Grulke 2021, J Cheminform 13:92, DOI 10.1186/s13321-021-00571-7]** Noise added to QM9 is a controlled stress test, not a simulation of measurement.
- **Nobody has published a Student-t fit or a mixture fit to bioactivity assay error.** Every fit in the literature is a Gaussian to a truncated core, plus Krüger and Overington's Laplace to a difference distribution. That is a genuine gap and a contribution this paper can make. **[INFERENCE from the exhaustive search across all five reports.]**

## PART 2 — THE AXES THAT MATTER

From Part 1, label error varies along four genuinely distinct axes and one that turned out to be a mirage.

### Axis 1 — Tail shape: how often is the error very large?

**Supported.** Section 1.2 and 1.3. A Gaussian under-produces errors beyond one log unit by a factor of 2.3 **[COMPUTED from Landrum's printed fractions]**, and the far tail is 14 orders of magnitude too improbable under a Gaussian **[COMPUTED from Kalliokoski's maximum]**. Anderson-Darling rejects normality at p < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ **[READ]**.

### Axis 2 — Does the error size vary between molecules, and if so, can it be predicted from the molecule?

**Supported, and this is really two questions.** Error size demonstrably varies between molecules: 2.4-fold across cytotoxicity systems **[READ]**, 3.6-fold between solubility test subsets **[READ]**, 3-fold between laboratories for solubility **[READ]**, 3–4-fold between within-laboratory and between-laboratory potency **[READ]**.

The important distinction is whether that variation is *visible in the molecular structure*. Aggregation is predictable from calculated logP and structural similarity **[READ]**. Between-laboratory variation is not visible in the structure at all. These two cases matter differently for a paper about uncertainty estimates: a model can in principle learn the first and cannot learn the second. **[INFERENCE, but grounded — Heid et al. 2023 state exactly this: mean-variance estimation works "if the error is a function of the input features" and is "not amenable to noise that is uniformly distributed over all data points or systematic noise that is applied based on external factors not represented in the input features". READ, Heid et al. 2023, J Chem Inf Model 63(13):4012–4029, DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.3c00373]**

Note the mathematical link between Axis 1 and Axis 2. A Student-t distribution is exactly a Gaussian whose width is itself random and drawn fresh for every point. **[READ, Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, 2006, §2.3.7, equation 2.158: "Student's t-distribution is obtained by adding up an infinite number of Gaussian distributions having the same mean but different precisions."]** So "heavy-tailed" and "unpredictably varying error size" are the same thing described two ways. The design should exploit that rather than duplicate it.

### Axis 3 — Is the error a smooth perturbation, or is a minority of labels simply wrong?

**Supported.** Section 1.4. Above 2.5 log units essentially every disagreement is an outright database error **[READ, Kalliokoski Table 2]**. Hampel's empirical statement on gross errors in scientific routine data is that "their fraction is typically between 1 percent and 10 percent". **[READ, Hampel 2001, ETH Zürich Research Report No. 94]** This is a distinct axis because it decouples *how many* records are wrong from *how wrong* they are. A single smooth distribution cannot do that, which is exactly what Part 1.3 demonstrated numerically.

### Axis 4 — Is the error symmetric, or one-sided?

**Supported.** Section 1.7 for censoring (up to 63% of records). Section 1.6 for aggregation, which only ever makes a compound look more potent. All six current strategies average to zero and cannot represent either. **[INFERENCE, verified against the code.]**

### The axis the design currently assumes, which the evidence does not support

**Does the error depend on the value of the label?** **No.** This was tested directly on 20,356 independent measurement pairs and rejected, including the specific solubility-limit hypothesis. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013]** Industry precision statistics assume the opposite. **[READ, Assay Guidance Manual; Kramer et al. 2016]** No primary measurement of a value-dependent error law exists for any of the endpoints in question. **[NOT VERIFIED — absent.]**

Four of the six current strategies — value-proportional, heteroscedastic, threshold and quantile — differ from each other only in *how* they make the error depend on the label value. On this axis they are four flavours of an unsupported hypothesis. **[INFERENCE.]**

The citation currently used to justify them does not support them. The manuscript at `paper.tex:186` cites Heid et al. 2023 for structure-dependent noise. Heid et al. did not measure structure-dependent noise; they **imposed** it, as a hand-chosen two-level step — 20 kcal/mol for nitrogen-containing molecules and 2 kcal/mol for others in one experiment, and keyed to the **sign** of the enthalpy in the other. **[READ, Heid et al. 2023]** Neither is a proportional or square-root scaling with the label magnitude. That citation should be removed from that sentence.

There is a second, independent objection that does not depend on any literature. The formula at `rust/src/main.rs:313`, `adaptive_sigma = base_sigma * (1.0 + proportionality_factor * value.abs())`, carries a hidden constant with units of "per electronvolt". Run the same target in Hartree and the strategy changes completely. A physical error law cannot depend on your choice of unit. **[INFERENCE, but not really arguable.]**

## PART 3 — THE PROPOSED NOISE TYPES

Four arms plus one optional fifth. Notation: `y_i` is the true label of molecule i, `ε_i` is the perturbation added to it, `τ` is the target dose (Part 4), `N` is the number of training molecules.

---

### Arm A — Plain Gaussian

**Plain English.** Every molecule's label is nudged by a random amount of the same typical size. Small nudges are common, large ones rare. This is the baseline everyone else uses.

**Definition.** `ε_i ~ Normal(0, σ²)`, drawn independently for each molecule.

**Parameters.** One: `σ`. Dose is `σ` exactly.

**Real mechanism.** Repeat measurements in one laboratory with one protocol. Kalliokoski's own control compounds sit here, at standard deviation 0.17 to 0.22 log units. **[READ]**

**Why it is in.** It is the reference point, and it is what the two direct predecessors used. Kolmar and Grulke: noise was "sampling from a Gaussian distribution of zero mean and increasing standard deviation". **[READ]** Cortés-Ciriano et al. 2015: "The noise was simulated by sampling from Gaussian distributions with increasingly larger variances". **[READ, abstract, J Chem Inf Model 55(7):1413–1425, DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.5b00101]** Without this arm nothing else is interpretable.

**Status: KEEPS the current `legacy` strategy unchanged.**

---

### Arm B — Student-t (the heavy-tailed arm)

**Plain English.** Same as Arm A, but occasional labels get badly mangled far more often than a Gaussian allows. Equivalently: every molecule gets a Gaussian nudge, but the *size* of that nudge is itself random and sometimes enormous.

**Definition.** `ε_i = s · T_i`, where `T_i` is a standard Student-t random variable with `ν` degrees of freedom, drawn independently. The variance is `s² · ν/(ν−2)`, defined only for `ν > 2`. **[READ, Probability Distribution Explorer, Student-t; and Bishop 2006 §2.3.7 for the Gaussian-mixture identity]**

**Parameters.** Two: the scale `s` (set by the dose-matching rule in Part 4) and the degrees of freedom `ν`, which is the tail knob. **Recommended settings: ν = 10, 5, 3.** As `ν` grows the distribution becomes a Gaussian, so Arm A is the `ν = ∞` end of this same axis — the experiment nests cleanly on one scalar. **[READ, Bishop: "in the limit ν → ∞ the t-distribution... becomes a Gaussian"]**

**Why Student-t and not Laplace or a mixture.**
1. It has **one knob** that moves tail weight monotonically, so results plot on a single axis. Laplace has a fixed excess kurtosis of 3, giving one point rather than an axis. **[COMPUTED — Laplace excess kurtosis 3.0 confirmed against `scipy.stats`; it equals Student-t with exactly 6 degrees of freedom.]**
2. It has a **closed-form variance**, so dose-matching is one line of algebra.
3. It has a **top-tier robust-modelling citation**: "The degrees of freedom parameter of the t distribution provides a convenient dimension for achieving robust statistical inference." **[READ, Lange, Little & Taylor 1989, JASA 84(408):881–896, abstract]**
4. It is **mechanistically the right story**: it is exactly a Gaussian whose per-measurement precision varies **[READ, Bishop equation 2.158]**, which is what Kalliokoski's within-laboratory versus between-laboratory gap and Landrum's curation effect both describe.

**Honest limitation, stated up front.** Part 1.3 showed that matching the real tail requires about 1.1 degrees of freedom, which has no variance and cannot be dose-matched. **[COMPUTED]** So Arm B at ν = 3 is a *conservative* heavy-tailed arm — real data is heavier. That is why Arm D exists. Say this in the paper; a reviewer who checks will find it anyway.

**Status: NEW. This is the arm the user asked about, and it is the single most defensible addition.**

---

### Arm C — Grouped scale (the honest replacement for "heteroscedastic")

**Plain English.** Some groups of related molecules came from a less reliable source, and every molecule in such a group gets a bigger error. Which groups those are is fixed and, in principle, visible in the chemistry.

**Definition.** Partition the training molecules into groups by Bemis–Murcko scaffold cluster — the same clustering already used for the scaffold splits, and already loadable from `scaffold_assignments_{seed}.json` at `rust/src/main.rs:280`. Mark each group unreliable independently with probability `q`. Then

```
ε_i ~ Normal(0, σ_i²)     with σ_i = σ_high if molecule i is in an unreliable group,
                                    σ_low  otherwise,      σ_high = λ · σ_low
```

**Parameters.** Two: the width ratio `λ`, and the fraction of *molecules* that end up in unreliable groups, call it `f` (which follows from `q` and the cluster sizes; compute `f` from the data, do not assume it equals `q`).

**Parameter values with actual sources.** `λ` between 3 and 3.6:
- Solubility: within-laboratory error "need to be multiplied by a factor of **3**" to reach between-laboratory error. **[READ, Avdeef 2019]**
- Solubility test sets: 0.62 / 0.17 = **3.6**. **[READ, Llinàs & Avdeef 2019 title]**
- Potency: 0.68 / 0.17–0.22 = **3.1 to 4.0**. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013]**

These three independent endpoints agree, and they land on the same value Tukey conventionally used for contaminated normals. **[READ, Tukey's λ = 3, via Gerstenberger & Vogel 2014, arXiv:1405.5027, §2.3]** Use **λ = 3** and cite all four. **There is no published number for `f`** — choose 0.2 and say so plainly. **[NOT VERIFIED.]**

**Real mechanism.** Merging data from laboratories or assays of different quality; compound classes with systematically worse measurements; colloidal aggregation, which selects victims by structural neighbourhood. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013; Avdeef 2019; Landrum & Riniker 2024; Irwin et al. 2015; Malo et al. 2006]**

**Why it is distinct from Arm B.** Both make the error size vary between molecules. In Arm B the variation is fresh per molecule and invisible in the inputs. In Arm C it is constant within a structural group and therefore learnable. Heid et al. state exactly why that distinction matters for uncertainty estimates: variance-estimating methods succeed "if the error is a function of the input features" and fail on noise "applied based on external factors not represented in the input features". **[READ]** This is the arm that tests whether the paper's uncertainty methods can find unreliable molecules.

**Status: REPLACES `heteroscedastic`, `value_proportional`, `threshold` and `quantile` — all four. It keeps the idea those were reaching for (error size varies between molecules) and fixes the part the evidence rejects (that it varies with the label value).**

---

### Arm D — Sparse contamination (the honest replacement for "outlier")

**Plain English.** Most labels are fine. A small number are simply wrong — a unit typed incorrectly, the wrong target, the wrong assay. Those labels bear no relation to the truth.

**Definition.** With probability `p`, molecule i is contaminated:

```
contaminated:      ε_i = R_i − y_i,   R_i drawn uniformly from the observed training-label range
not contaminated:  ε_i ~ Normal(0, σ₀²)
```

A simpler variant, if drawing a replacement is awkward in the Rust code: contaminated molecules get `ε_i ~ Normal(0, (λσ₀)²)` with `λ = 3`. That is Tukey's contaminated normal exactly. **[READ, Gerstenberger & Vogel 2014 §2.3, attributing the model and λ = 3 to Tukey 1960; the Tukey chapter itself was NOT obtainable and is cited here secondarily.]**

**Parameters.** Two: the contaminated fraction `p` and the contaminant scale (either the replacement range, or `λ`).

**Parameter values with sources.** `p` in {0.01, 0.05, 0.10}. Justification: "for scientific routine data, not taken with utmost care, their fraction is typically between 1 percent and 10 percent". **[READ, Hampel 2001]** This is the only empirically grounded statement I could find for the contaminated fraction; the familiar 1/5/10 percent grid has no other provenance and should not be presented as canonical. **[INFERENCE.]** Supporting from within cheminformatics: 13% of IC50 pairs still differ by more than a full log unit even after maximal curation **[READ, Landrum & Riniker 2024]**, and essentially all pairs above 2.5 log units are database errors **[READ, Kalliokoski Table 2]**.

**Real mechanism.** Unit-transcription errors, wrong receptor subtype, cell-based versus biochemical assay confusion, undifferentiated stereochemistry, retracted papers. All named explicitly. **[READ, Kalliokoski 2013 Table 2; Kramer et al. 2012 abstract]**

**Why it is not redundant with Arm B.** It decouples the number of bad labels from how bad they are — two knobs where Student-t has one. Part 1.3 showed that a single smooth family cannot reproduce the real tail, so both knobs are needed. **[COMPUTED]** It is also the formal home of Huber's contamination model, `F = (1−ε)Φ + εH`, which a statistically literate reviewer will look for. **[READ, Huber 1964, Ann Math Statist 35(1):73–101, DOI 10.1214/aoms/1177703732 — abstract read directly; the mixture formula is quoted here from van Garderen & Schluter, arXiv:2502.09145 §2.3, and from Chen, Gao & Ren, arXiv:1511.04144, both READ.]**

**Status: REPLACES `outlier`. It keeps the contamination idea and drops the one part that has no support — selecting which molecules to corrupt by whether their label is extreme. Real contamination is not label-selected; it is a property of the record, not of the value.**

### Arm E (optional) — One-sided censoring

**Plain English.** Some measurements fall outside the assay's working range. Instead of a number, the assay produces "greater than X". Databases and curation pipelines then record that as an exact number, or throw it away.

**Definition.** Pick a threshold `c` at the (1−p)-th percentile of the training labels. For every molecule with `y_i > c`, set the recorded label to `c`. Left-censoring is the mirror image.

**Parameters.** One: the censored fraction `p`, swept over {0.10, 0.25, 0.40}. Sweep the fraction, not a dose, because Svensson and colleagues found the effect switches on between 13% and 35% censoring. **[READ]**

**Real mechanism.** Assay dynamic range. Verbatim from the Assay Guidance Manual: values outside the tested range "should be '<Xmin' or '>Xmax', as appropriate." **[READ, NCBI Bookshelf NBK91994]**

**Why it might be worth including.** It is the most common real error mechanism by prevalence — 25% to 63% of labels in ten of fifteen real industrial assays, including 8% in a logD assay of 88,114 compounds. **[READ, Svensson et al. 2025, Table 1]** It is also the only arm that is not symmetric, so it tests something no other arm can. And it bears directly on uncertainty estimates: that paper's whole result is that models ignoring censoring produce worse uncertainty, with mean-squared-error improvements "on average, around 20%" on assays above 35% censoring. **[READ]**

**Why it is flagged optional.** It cannot be dose-matched against the other four, because it has no variance parameter and does not average to zero. It has to be reported as a separate axis with its own figure. That is real cost. **[INFERENCE.]**

**Status: NEW and optional.**

## PART 4 — HOW TO MAKE THEM COMPARABLE

### 4.1 The quantity to hold constant

**Hold the dose constant.** Define the dose as the root-mean-square perturbation actually delivered to the training labels:

```
D  =  sqrt(  (1/N) · Σ_i  E[ε_i²]  )
```

For zero-mean noise this is `sqrt( (1/N) Σ_i σ_i² )`. Report it in the label's own units. **The knob you turn in the command line is an implementation detail; D is the dose.** Two arms are comparable only at equal D.

**Why this quantity, defended.**

1. The outcome metrics — root-mean-square error and R² — are second-moment quantities. Equal second moment in the noise is what makes them comparable across arms. **[INFERENCE, but it is the direct reason.]**
2. It is what the field's precedent effectively fixes. Kolmar and Grulke put the delivered standard deviation on the x-axis: "the x-axis is the standard deviation (σ) of the Gaussian distribution of the added error". **[READ]** With a single shape they never had to distinguish the knob from the dose. With six shapes you do.
3. Zhao and colleagues fix noise as a multiple of a physical standard deviation: "the standard deviation of each data set was multiplied by a parameter k (k = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0)". **[READ, Zhao, Wang, Sedykh & Zhu 2017, ACS Omega 2(6):2805–2812, DOI 10.1021/acsomega.7b00274]** That is a delivered dose, not an index.
4. The alternative convention from the machine-learning label-noise literature — fixing the *fraction* of corrupted labels — does not transfer. Song and colleagues define noise by "a noise rate τ ∈ [0,1]" and then state explicitly that "in this survey, we focus on the classification setting". **[READ, Song et al. 2023, IEEE TNNLS 34(11):8135–8153, arXiv:2007.08199, §II-B and §II-D]** A flipped discrete label has no magnitude; a perturbed continuous label is nothing but magnitude.

### 4.3 The general recipe

Every arm can be written as a per-molecule scale map, `σ_i = s · g_i`, where `s` is the single knob and `g_i` is the shape function. Then:

```
Unit dose of the shape:   G  =  sqrt( (1/N) · Σ_i  g_i² )
Dose at any knob value:   D(s)  =  s · G           (exact — the map is linear in s)
Knob for a target dose τ: s*  =  τ / G
```

Compute `G` once per (dataset, split, arm, arm-parameters) from the **training labels only**, then rescale. Write `G` and the realised `D` out to the results CSV so every figure can be traced to them.

### 4.4 Arm-by-arm algebra

**Arm A, Gaussian.** `g_i = 1`, so `G = 1` and `σ = τ`.

**Arm B, Student-t.** Variance is `s²·ν/(ν−2)`, so

```
s*  =  τ · sqrt( (ν − 2) / ν )
```

| Degrees of freedom ν | Scale `s*/τ` | Median perturbation (dose 1) | Fraction beyond 2× dose | Fraction beyond 3× dose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.5774 | 0.442 | 0.0405 | 0.01385 |
| 5 | 0.7746 | 0.563 | 0.0493 | 0.01173 |
| 10 | 0.8944 | 0.626 | 0.0493 | 0.00732 |
| ∞ (Gaussian) | 1.0000 | 0.674 | 0.0455 | 0.00270 |

**[all COMPUTED, verified against `scipy.stats`]**

**Arm C, grouped scale.** With a fraction `f` of molecules in unreliable groups and width ratio `λ`:

```
D²  =  σ_low² · ( 1 − f + f·λ² )
σ_low*  =  τ / sqrt( 1 − f + f·λ² )        σ_high*  =  λ · σ_low*
```

At `f = 0.2` and `λ = 3`: `σ_low* = 0.620·τ`, `σ_high* = 1.861·τ`. **[COMPUTED]** Compute `f` from the actual cluster assignment, do not assume it.

**Arm D, sparse contamination.** With a Gaussian contaminant of width `λσ₀`:

```
D²  =  σ₀² · ( 1 + p·(λ² − 1) )
σ₀*  =  τ / sqrt( 1 + p·(λ² − 1) )
```

| p | λ | `σ₀*/τ` | Contaminant width / τ | Excess kurtosis | Median perturbation (dose 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 3 | 0.9623 | 2.887 | 1.63 | 0.654 |
| 0.05 | 3 | 0.8452 | 2.535 | 4.65 | 0.592 |
| 0.10 | 3 | 0.7454 | 2.236 | 5.33 | 0.544 |

**[all COMPUTED; the kurtosis for p = 0.05, λ = 3 was additionally confirmed by simulation in report 3 — 4.636 simulated against 4.653 algebraic]**

For the replacement variant, `E[(R − y)²]` has no closed form; compute it directly over the training labels, then `σ₀* = sqrt( (τ² − p·E[(R−y)²]) / (1−p) )`. **Note the constraint:** replacement noise has a floor. If `p · E[(R−y)²] > τ²` the target dose is unreachable at that contamination fraction and you must lower `p` or raise `τ`. State that constraint in the methods rather than letting it silently clip.

**One useful non-monotonicity to be aware of.** With `λ = 3`, the kurtosis of the contaminated normal peaks near `p ≈ 0.10` and falls again by `p = 0.20`, because at large `p` the mixture becomes just a wider Gaussian. **[COMPUTED, confirmed in report 3]** Do not describe `p` as a monotone "tail heaviness" dial.

### 4.5 The undefined-variance case

Student-t has no finite variance at or below 2 degrees of freedom, and no finite mean at or below 1. **[READ, Probability Distribution Explorer, Student-t: variance is "νσ²/(ν−2)" for ν > 2, "infinite" for 1 < ν < 2, "undefined" for ν ≤ 1.]** Between 2 and 4 degrees of freedom the variance exists but the kurtosis does not. **[READ, arXiv:1912.01607: "the moments are undefined when k ≥ ν"]**

Three ways to handle it. Take the first.

1. **Restrict the design to ν > 2.** With ν ∈ {3, 5, 10} the variance always exists, ν = 3 already gives an infinite-kurtosis arm, and dose-matching is exact everywhere. **Recommended.**
2. **If a Cauchy-like arm is ever wanted, switch the invariant for that arm alone to the median absolute perturbation and say so explicitly.** The median is defined for every distribution including Cauchy. To set the median absolute perturbation to `m`, use `s = m / t.ppf(0.75, ν)`. Do not silently mix invariants across arms.
3. **Truncate the tail at a physical bound** (for example, the label range), which makes the variance finite by construction — but then the realised dose must be measured, not assumed.

**A related warning that must appear in the methods.** Equal dose does not mean equal typical perturbation. A dose-matched Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom moves the median molecule by 0.44 of the dose; a Gaussian moves it by 0.67. **[COMPUTED]** The heavy-tailed arm perturbs most molecules *less* and pays for it with a handful of huge errors. That is exactly the effect being isolated, but a reader will read "same σ" as "same perturbation per molecule" unless told otherwise. **Report the median absolute perturbation as an extra column next to the dose for every arm.**

## PART 5 — ANCHORING TO REALITY

### 5.1 Report the noise level three ways

Three numbers, side by side, for every point on every curve.

1. **The dose in the label's own units** — log units for the experimental sets, electronvolts for QM9. This is the anchoring axis for the experimental endpoints.
2. **The dose divided by the standard deviation of the labels.** Unitless, so electronvolts and log units land on one axis. This is the primary cross-dataset axis. It is exactly Zhao and colleagues' parameter `k`. **[READ]** Equivalently, it is one over the square root of the signal-to-noise ratio, the axis Hastie, Tibshirani and Tibshirani argue is the right one: `SNR = Var(f(x₀)) / Var(ε₀)`, and their central finding is that method rankings *reverse* along it. **[READ, arXiv:1707.08692, published Statistical Science 2020 35(4):579–592]**
3. **The dose as a multiple of the published assay error for that endpoint.** This is the strongest rhetorical move available and it is borrowed directly from Kolmar and Grulke: "1.1 log units of noise were added, or 1.6 times the average standard deviation reported in ChemBL." **[READ]**

**Do not use fraction-of-the-label-range as the primary axis**, even though both predecessors did. **[READ, Kolmar & Grulke equation 8; Cortés-Ciriano et al. 2015 abstract]** The range is set by two order statistics and one outlier moves it. Cite it as prior art; do not adopt it.

## PART 6 — WHAT THIS COSTS AND WHAT IT BUYS

| Arm | The question only it answers | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Gaussian** | How much accuracy is lost per unit of ordinary, well-behaved error? The reference every other arm is read against. | **Essential.** Cannot be cut. |
| **B — Student-t** | Does the *shape* of the error matter at all, once the amount is held fixed? Directly attacks the assumption Kolmar and Grulke made and conceded was "certainly not representative of every real-world data situation". **[READ]** | **Essential.** This is the arm the user asked about and the paper's clearest novel contribution — no one has published a Student-t noise study on bioactivity data. |
| **C — Grouped scale** | Can a model's uncertainty estimate find *which* molecules are unreliable, when unreliability is a property of the chemistry? Heid et al. show this is exactly where variance-estimating methods succeed or fail. **[READ]** | **Essential.** Without it the paper has no arm where the noise pattern is learnable from the inputs, which is a central claim about uncertainty methods. |
| **D — Sparse contamination** | Does it matter whether the same total error is delivered as many small mistakes or a few catastrophic ones? Part 1.3 shows a smooth heavy tail cannot reach the real tail, so this arm is not redundant with B. | **Essential, but reducible.** If cost forces a cut, run **one** setting (p = 0.05, λ = 3) as an anchor to Huber and Tukey rather than a full sweep. |
| **E — Censoring** | What happens when the error is one-sided and the model is never told? The only arm that is not zero-mean. Most prevalent real mechanism by a wide margin — up to 63% of labels. **[READ]** | **Nice to have.** Cut it first. It cannot be dose-matched with the others, needs its own figure and its own axis, and it is a defensible standalone follow-up paper on its own. |

**Reduced set if the compute budget is tight: A, B (three ν settings), C (one setting), D (one setting).** That is six configurations per σ per dataset instead of the current six strategies, so the run cost is unchanged while the design becomes defensible.

**The strongest single sentence the redesigned experiment lets you write** — assuming the result comes out that way — is that shape matters at fixed dose, which no published QSAR noise study has tested. Kolmar and Grulke used one shape. Cortés-Ciriano and colleagues used one shape. Zhao and colleagues used one shape. **[all READ]** No published QSAR study injecting multiple noise shapes and comparing them at matched dose was found by any of the five searches. **[INFERENCE from absence — this is absence of evidence, not proof of absence.]**

## PART 7 — HONEST GAPS

**What this design still cannot test.**

1. **Correlated error across molecules from a shared physical cause.** Arm C makes the error *size* shared within a group, but the errors are still drawn independently. Real plate and laboratory effects add the *same* offset to every compound in a block — the standard high-throughput screening model is a row effect plus a column effect plus a residual. **[READ, Malo et al. 2006]** That is a mean shift on a subpopulation, and no zero-mean arm produces it. Kalliokoski's +0.355 log unit systematic offset between pKi and pIC50 measurements is the same kind of thing. **[READ]** Adding a block-offset arm is the most defensible future extension.

2. **One-sided artefacts,** unless Arm E is included. Aggregation only ever makes a compound look more potent; 95% of the actives in a 70,563-compound screen were aggregation artefacts. **[READ, Feng et al. 2007]** Solubility-limited ceilings only ever push the other way. Neither is representable as symmetric noise.

3. **Error selected by structure in the way the literature actually documents it.** Arm C uses scaffold clusters as a proxy. The real mechanism is calculated logP above 3 plus similarity to a known aggregator. **[READ, Irwin et al. 2015]** Implementing that faithfully needs an aggregator reference set that is not in this repository.

4. **Duplicated records that look independent.** 90% of apparent replicate pairs in ChEMBL are the same measurement re-cited. **[READ, Kramer 2012 abstract]** This means the *apparent* noise level in a pooled dataset is itself mis-estimated. No noise-injection scheme reproduces it.

5. **Distribution shift over time.** Orthogonal to label noise entirely. Random splits give an optimistic R² compared with time-based splits. **[READ, Sheridan 2013, J Chem Inf Model 53(4):783–790, DOI 10.1021/ci400084k, abstract]**

6. **Whether real error is heavier than Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom.** Part 1.3 says yes — matching the tail needs about 1.1 degrees of freedom **[COMPUTED]** — but that region cannot be dose-matched, so the design deliberately stops short of the real tail. The paper should say so.
