## PART 1 — SOURCES

### 1.1 Direct test of potency-dependence of IC50 error — the single most relevant study

**Kalliokoski T, Kramer C, Vulpetti A, Gedeck P. "Comparability of mixed IC50 data – a statistical analysis." PLoS ONE 2013;8(4):e61007. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0061007. PMID 23613770 / PMC3628986. Open access.**

- **What was measured (R):** pairwise differences (ΔpIC50) between *independent* IC50 measurements of the same protein–ligand system in ChEMBL.
- **Sample size (R):** "From the initially available 616.555 IC50 values with confidence score greater or equal to four 10.895 IC50 values for 3.480 Protein/Ligand systems remained, yielding 20.356 pairs of independent measurements." The ΔpIC50 distribution figure uses "16.844 pairs".
- **Error magnitude (R):** σ_pIC50 = 0.87 raw; **σ_pIC50 = 0.68**, MUE = 0.55, MedUE = 0.43 after the √2 paired correction. In-house intra-lab/inter-day comparison: "The standard deviation of the pIC50 values are σ = 0.22 for rolipram/PDE4D and σ = 0.17 for cilostamide/PDE3."
- **Value-dependence — the key result (R), verified independently via both the PLOS HTML and the PMC copy:**
  > "We checked whether the ΔpIC50 depends on the overall activity measured or on physicochemical ligand properties like logP, logD, molecular weight (MW), polar surface area (PSA), the number hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), the number hydrogen bond donors (HBD) or the number of rotatable bonds. Boxplots of all those properties versus the ΔpIC50 are shown in Figure 6. **The ΔpIC50's depend neither on the average measured pIC50 nor on any of the ligand properties examined.**"

  > "One might assume that higher IC50 values show a larger variability than for example single digit µM IC50 values because of solubility limits. **However, our analysis shows that on the average this is clearly not the case.**"

This is a direct, adequately powered, explicit test of exactly the hypothesis behind the two strategies — and it is negative.

### 1.5 Heid E, McGill CJ, Vermeire FH, Green WH. "Characterizing Uncertainty in Machine Learning for Chemistry." J Chem Inf Model 2023;63(13):4012–4029. DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.3c00373. PMID 37338239.

This is the `Heid2023` entry at `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/citations.bib:683` and the citation at `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/paper.tex:186`. **I read the full text** (MIT DSpace open-access copy; local text at `/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-apunt-repos-qsar-qm-models/28450b4e-4a9e-4197-993c-548e1dd48b09/scratchpad/heid.txt`).

**What it actually says (R):**

> "In general, noise can be of random, uniform nature (homoscedastic), afflicting all data points with the same error probability distribution, or systematic (heteroscedastic), where different domains of data are affected by different error probability distribution."

> "**Systematic Noise.** If different regions of chemical space lead to larger noise levels, it is possible for a model to learn which regions are unreliable if the loss function is adapted accordingly, as first reported by Nix et al."

> "Mean-variance estimation and similar techniques can be very successful in training models on noisy data sets **if the error is a function of the input features**, since it allows the model to learn on which data points to focus and which to regard as unreliable. However, it is not amenable to noise that is uniformly distributed over all data points or systematic noise that is applied based on external factors not represented in the input features of the training data."

**Their two systematic-noise experiments (R) — both entirely synthetic, imposed by the authors on a noise-free group-additivity enthalpy dataset (711,613 training points):**

> "In the first case (Figure 3, violet), we apply Gaussian noise of standard deviation 20 kcal/mol for nitrogen-containing molecules and Gaussian noise of standard deviation 2 kcal/mol for non-nitrogen-containing molecules."

> "In the second case (Figure 3, orange), we apply a Gaussian noise of standard deviation 20 kcal/mol for molecules with positive enthalpy and Gaussian noise of standard deviation 2 kcal/mol for molecules with negative enthalpy."

Results (R): case 1 — MVE recovers 2.35 kcal/mol predicted SD vs 2.12 RMSE (non-N) and 20.0 vs 20.0 (N-containing). Case 2 — ensembling fails to separate regimes (10.6 vs 11.5 kcal/mol), MVE succeeds (2.4 vs 19.5).

**Their homoscedastic-shape result (R), which is directly relevant to the user's whole noise-distribution axis:**

> "We did not observe any difference in overall model performance between different error distributions, as long as the mean and standard deviation of the noise was the same, respectively. Though noise distributions found in real data may be non-Gaussian, if homoscedastic, they should still follow the same trends of approaching an asymptote due to noise."

**Assessment of the paper.tex:186 claim (I).** The sentence in the manuscript — "\citet{Heid2023} used mean-variance estimation and bias-variance decomposition to show that noise can be tied to specific modalities, for example structure dependence" — **overstates the source.** Heid et al. did *not* show that real noise is structure-dependent. They *stipulated* structure-dependent noise (a hand-imposed two-level step function keyed to "contains nitrogen") and then showed that **MVE can recover the regimes they injected**. It is a demonstration of a *method's* capability, not an empirical finding about experimental error. Their second case is keyed to the **sign** of the label, not its magnitude — a step, not a proportional or square-root scaling. This citation therefore does **not** support the two formulas in the pipeline.

### 1.6 The mechanism that *is* well documented: assay dynamic range → **censoring**, not smooth heteroscedasticity

**Sebaugh JL. "Guidelines for accurate EC50/IC50 estimation." Pharm Stat 2011;10(2):128–134. DOI 10.1002/pst.426. PMID 22328315.**
- (R, abstract): "The relative EC50/IC50 should only be used if there are at least two assay concentrations beyond the lower and upper bend points. The absolute EC50/IC50 should only be used if there are at least two assay concentrations whose predicted response is less than 50% and two whose predicted response is greater than 50%."

**Assay Guidance Manual, "Assay Operations for SAR Support" (Beck B, Chen Y-F, Dere W, Devanarayan V, Eastwood BJ, Farmen MW, Iturria SJ, Iversen PW, Kahl SD, Moore RA, Sawyer BD, Weidner J; 2012, updated 2017). NCBI Bookshelf NBK91994.**
- (R): "There should be at least one point on both sides of the reported IC50, i.e. the reported IC50 should lie inside the concentration-range used in the assay."
- (R): "Cases not satisfying this rule should not have an IC50 reported or reported with a comment that indicates the value is extrapolated. If a value is reported, it should be '<Xmin' or '>Xmax', as appropriate."
- (R) MSR (Minimum Significant Ratio) is computed "where *s* is the standard deviation of the **log**-Ki/Kb/EC50 values"; "two controls permit direct calculation of both the within-run and overall MSR's, and a check that the MSR is consistent over a range of potencies." Overall MSR standard: "should be less than or equal to 7.5", with chemistry often demanding "2-3".
- **This is decisive for the question (I):** industry's own precision statistic is a **constant fold-ratio**, i.e. a **constant SD in log units**, and the QC expectation is that it is *consistent across potencies*. Outside the working range the assay does not produce a noisier number — it produces **no number**, only a `>` or `<` qualifier.

### 1.7 Log-normality: why the raw-scale heteroscedasticity vanishes in the modelling space

**Srinivasan B, Lloyd MD. "Quantitation and Error Measurements in Dose–Response Curves." J Med Chem 2025;68(3):2052–2056. DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5c00131. Open access (CC-BY, University of Bath repository) — read in full.**

> "It comes as a revelation to many when they appreciate that most biological data are lognormally distributed… (i.e., EC50, IC50, Ki or Km) the values follow a lognormal distribution. Also, IC50 measures estimated for the same compound across different replicate runs or IC50 estimates for different compounds follow a lognormal distribution."

> "In this kind of distribution, **the trait varies multiplicatively (doubling or halving)**."

> "Hence, transforming the values on a lognormal distribution by taking their logarithms **converts multiplicative variability about the measure of a central tendency to arithmetic variability**. Thus, this transformation makes the distribution normal or Gaussian…"

> "Once this transformation has been done, we can take the approach of computing arithmetic mean from replicate measures and report the error as per standard practise of ± standard deviations (SD) or ± standard error of the mean (SEM)."

Also (R), on the raw signal domain: serial dilution gives "accumulation of errors, **poorer % cross-validation (CV) at lower drug concentrations**".

(The companion editorial, Srinivasan & Lloyd, J Med Chem 2024;67(20):17931–17934, DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.4c02052, I did **not** read — NOT VERIFIED.)

**Horwitz W, Albert R. "The Horwitz Ratio (HorRat): A Useful Index of Method Performance with Respect to Precision." J AOAC Int 2006;89(4):1095ff. — read in full.**

> "Although it had been observed that high concentrations had low RSDR, and low concentrations had high values, this impression had not been developed quantitatively. This change in RSDR with concentration could be represented empirically by what is now known as the Horwitz equation
> **RSDR, % = 2C^–0.15**
> or its equivalent,
> **SR = 0.02 C^0.85**
> where the concentration, C, is expressed as a dimensionless mass fraction…"

> "The basic observation was that the among-laboratory precision, expressed as RSDR, **doubled for every decrease of 2 orders of magnitude in concentration**, C, expressed as a mass fraction."

This is the strongest, best-established quantitative heteroscedasticity law in analytical chemistry — but note carefully what it says (I): the **absolute** SD is a near-linear power of concentration (exponent 0.85), i.e. approximately **constant relative error**, i.e. **approximately constant SD once you take logs**. Horwitz *supports* the constant-σ-in-log-space model, it does not support additive value-scaling on a log-scale label.

## PART 2 — ANSWERS

### A) Is there solid evidence for value-dependent error in these endpoints?

**No — for potency (IC50/Ki), the best evidence is explicitly negative. Mixed-to-absent elsewhere.**

- The only study I found that *directly tested* it (Kalliokoski 2013, 20,356 independent measurement pairs) tested it and rejected it, including the specific solubility-limit hypothesis: "The ΔpIC50's depend neither on the average measured pIC50 nor on any of the ligand properties examined."
- Industry practice encodes the *opposite* assumption: MSR is a constant fold-ratio, computed from the SD of **log** potency, and QC checks that it is "consistent over a range of potencies" (Assay Guidance Manual).
- Kramer 2016 (AstraZeneca, 2005–2014, all assays) reports uncertainty as "less than a twofold difference" — a ratio, i.e. constant in log space.
- For logD/logP and Caco-2 I found **no primary source at all** reporting a measured error-versus-value relationship. The one quantitative heteroskedasticity result in logP (arXiv:2512.24643) is model residual variance on a *computed* descriptor, not experimental error, and is an unrefereed preprint.
- The only value-dependence claim I found anywhere in an ADME endpoint (CLint variability greater for metabolically stable compounds, per Cyprotex via Fagerholm's preprint) is secondary, unquantified, and for a different endpoint.

What **is** solidly established is a different thing: **relative error grows as the raw concentration falls** (Horwitz: RSD_R = 2C^−0.15, "doubled for every decrease of 2 orders of magnitude in concentration"). But that is *multiplicative* error on the raw scale, which is exactly what the log transform removes: "transforming the values on a lognormal distribution by taking their logarithms converts multiplicative variability… to arithmetic variability" (Srinivasan & Lloyd 2025). Since pIC50, logD, logP, logS and log-Papp are **already the modelling space**, the documented raw-scale heteroscedasticity has already been absorbed. (I)

### B) What functional form does the evidence support?

Ranked by strength of evidence:

1. **Constant σ in log space** (homoscedastic on pIC50 / logD / logS / log Papp). Supported by Kalliokoski's direct test, by MSR-as-a-constant-ratio, by Kramer 2016's "twofold" summary, by OECD 107's flat ±0.3 log criterion, and derivable from Horwitz + log-normality. **This is the defensible default.**
2. **Step change at the edge of the working range — implemented as censoring, not variance inflation.** Outside the tested concentration range the assay yields a qualifier (`>Xmax`, `<Xmin`), not a noisier number (Assay Guidance Manual; Sebaugh 2011; Lind 2010). For Caco-2, low-recovery/high-lipophilicity compounds are simply *dropped* ("not detectable and low recovery"). The right model of assay-limit effects is **truncation/censoring plus non-random missingness**, which is a different generative mechanism from "same y, bigger σ".
3. **Larger at both extremes:** no primary support found for experimental error. The only "funnel" evidence is model-residual variance on computed logP.
4. **Proportional to |y| or ∝√|y| on a log-scale label:** **no support found in any source.**

### C) Is the strongest form of "structured" error actually value-dependence?

**No. Value-dependence is the weakest structure of the candidates. The strongest, in descending order of evidential support:**

1. **Assay / laboratory / data source.** Landrum & Riniker 2024: going from minimal to maximal *curation* (i.e. controlling assay provenance) cut MAE from 0.50 → 0.27 and the fraction differing by >1 log unit from 27% → 13%. Kalliokoski 2013: inter-lab σ 0.68 vs intra-lab σ 0.17–0.22 — **a ~3–4× variance-relevant gap driven entirely by provenance**. Fagerholm: Caco-2 interlab ~3–4-fold vs intralab ~2-fold; the paper's headline is "Interlaboratory variability… on average about twice as high as intralaboratory variability."
2. **Chemical series / local SAR discontinuity.** Sheridan 2020: "activity cliff metrics determine predictivity better than the other metrics we investigated, whatever the type of dataset." Heid 2023's own only *demonstrable* structure was a chemistry-class flag (nitrogen-containing), i.e. a structural subpopulation.
3. **Position relative to a decision threshold** (Mervin 2021; Fagerholm's contradictory efflux-ratio classifications) — a threshold effect on *interpretation*, not on σ.
4. **Censoring at assay limits** — a missingness/truncation mechanism.
5. **Value magnitude** — directly tested and rejected for potency; unsupported elsewhere.

For the manuscript, this is a substantive finding: **the `scaffold` strategy already in `rust/src/main.rs` (structure/split-based σ) and an assay/batch-block strategy are the literature-supported ones; `value_proportional` and `heteroscedastic` are not.** (I)

### D) Do the two implemented formulas have any support? — **No.**

I confirmed both formulas in the source (read-only):

- `rust/src/main.rs:313` — `let adaptive_sigma = base_sigma * (1.0 + proportionality_factor * value.abs());` with default `proportionality_factor` = 0.1 (`rust/src/main.rs:1177`) → **σᵢ = σ·(1 + 0.1·|y|)**
- `rust/src/main.rs:413` — `let sigma = (alpha + beta * value.abs()).sqrt();` with `alpha = σ²·0.1`, `beta = σ²·0.05` (`rust/src/main.rs:1214–1216`) → **σᵢ = σ·√(0.1 + 0.05·|y|)**

Why they are unsupported:

1. **They are not scale-invariant — they are unit-dependent.** `1 + 0.1·|y|` has a hidden dimensional constant: 0.1 carries units of 1/eV. Run the same target in Hartree instead of eV and the strategy changes completely. A real error law cannot depend on your choice of unit. Horwitz's law is dimensionally honest because C is a dimensionless mass fraction.
2. **They are anchored at y = 0, which is physically arbitrary here.** Both formulas make σ minimal at |y| = 0. For a HOMO–LUMO gap, zero is not a special point (and `|y|` is meaningless since gaps are positive). For pIC50 or logD, zero is a unit convention (1 M; equal partition), not a measurement floor.
3. **They apply a raw-scale multiplicative model to an already-logged quantity.** Horwitz-type behaviour justifies σ ∝ (raw concentration)^0.85 — which becomes **constant σ in log units**. Applying a further proportional scaling on top of a log-scale label double-counts the transform. (I)
4. **They contradict the one direct empirical test.** Kalliokoski et al. looked for exactly this and found nothing.
5. **Heid et al. 2023 does not authorise them.** Heid's systematic noise is a hand-imposed **two-level step** on a categorical structural flag (contains N) or on the **sign** of the label — not a proportional or square-root magnitude scaling. Citing Heid at `paper.tex:186` as evidence for these formulas is a misreading of the source.
6. **The parameters change the noise dose, not just its shape — so the strategies are not comparable at matched σ.** (C) Over the real QM9 HOMO–LUMO gap distribution (n = 133,885 from `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/data/QM9/raw/gdb9.sdf.csv`, converted Hartree→eV at 27.2113862): gap min 0.669, max 16.928, mean 6.833, SD 1.293 eV. Therefore:
   - `value_proportional`: σᵢ/σ ranges **1.067 → 2.693**, mean 1.683, **RMS 1.688**. It injects ~69% more noise than nominal σ, with only a 2.5× spread across the whole dataset.
   - `heteroscedastic`: σᵢ/σ ranges **0.365 → 0.973**, mean 0.663, **RMS 0.665**. It injects **~34% less** noise than nominal σ everywhere — σᵢ < σ for *every molecule in QM9*.
   
   So at a nominal σ, "valprop" is a 1.69× dose and "hetero" a 0.67× dose — a **2.5× dose difference between two strategies that are supposed to be compared at the same σ**. Any strategy-vs-strategy robustness comparison is confounded by dose before the shape of the heteroscedasticity is even considered. (These figures independently reproduce the per-strategy RMS doses recorded in the project memory: ValProp 1.701, Hetero 0.669 — the small difference is the 10,000-molecule subset vs the full 133,885.)

**Blunt summary:** the two formulas encode a hypothesis that the best available evidence tested and rejected, using dimensionally inconsistent parameters that were not chosen from any measurement, and whose net effect on the experiments is a 2.5× uncontrolled difference in noise dose. They should be reported as *illustrative parameterisations of a heteroscedastic mechanism*, not as calibrated models of experimental error — and the paper should not lean on Heid 2023 to justify them.

**Defensible alternatives if the heteroscedastic axis is kept (I):**
- Replace the value-scaling with a **z-score-based** or quantile-based multiplier so it is unit-free (the existing `quantile` and `outlier` strategies already are).
- Normalise every strategy to **equal RMS σ across the dataset**, so shape is varied at matched dose.
- Add a **lab/batch block** strategy (constant σ within a source, different σ between sources) — this is the structure with by far the strongest published support (Kalliokoski: 0.17–0.22 intra vs 0.68 inter; Landrum & Riniker: 0.50 → 0.27 by curation).
- Add a **censoring** strategy (truncate labels above/below a working range and mark them qualified) — this is what "assay dynamic range" actually does, per the Assay Guidance Manual and Sebaugh 2011.
