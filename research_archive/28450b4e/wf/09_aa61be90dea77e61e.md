A) NATURE MI GENERALIZABILITY PAPER (Ektefaie et al., Nat Mach Intell 6:1512–1524, 2024)

Journal page numbers verified against printed footers (PDF page n = journal page 1511+n).

A1. Benchmark conclusions change with the evaluation protocol
- Abstract, p.1512: "Benchmarks traditionally interrogate model generalizability by generating metadata- or sequence similarity-based train and test splits of input data before assessing model performance. Here we show that this approach **mischaracterizes model generalizability** by failing to consider the full spectrum of cross-split overlap, that is, similarity between train and test splits."
- p.1513: "MB splits cannot guarantee that high performance on the test set will transfer to a new molecular sequencing dataset. This is because MB partitioning does not control sequence similarity between data subsets. Model generalizability can be overestimated when training sequences are more similar to sequences in the test set than sequences in a new dataset."
- p.1513: "In SB splits, sequence similarity can be controlled. However, the generalizability of the model at similarity thresholds different from a handful of those tested during model benchmarking **remains unknown, resulting in an incomplete evaluation of the model**."
- p.1514 (the headline quantitative demonstration): "The accuracy of long short-term memory (LSTM) and CNN models **decreased by 50%** between family and superfamily splits in the TAPE benchmark's remote homology dataset. The cross-split overlap was lower for the superfamily (71%) compared to the family split (97%)."
- p.1514: "These findings show that existing molecular benchmarks capture only a few points on the SPC, providing a **myopic assessment** of model generalizability. Further, the observation that model performance diminishes when cross-split overlap decreases identifies an **overestimation of model performance by existing benchmarks**."
- p.1513: the concrete calibration numbers — "the MB family split from the remote homology dataset in TAPE had a cross-split overlap of 97% and can be instantiated at SP = 0.025. In another example, the temporal-based MB split of PDBBind used to train the Equibind model had a cross-split overlap of 76% and was generated at SP = 0.55."
- Fig. 2a, p.1515, gives the full protocol-sensitivity table: cross-split overlap 97% (TAPE family) / 71% (superfamily) / 81% (fold) / 48–54% (CASP12, CB513, TS115) / 6.8% (PEER subcellular localization) / 70–91% (PDBBind variants). Same task, different published split ⇒ wildly different overlap ⇒ different conclusion.

A2. What they say about split strategy
- p.1518 (Discussion): "Existing SB and MB splits are attractive because they are computationally inexpensive. However, as shown in this paper, the time saved in computation leads to a mischaracterization of model generalizability. **Model evaluation should be seen as a step in model development as computationally expensive as model training, not an afterthought to generate a performance metric.**"
- p.1518: relevance to small-molecule work stated explicitly — "SPECTRA can be used with models beyond the molecular sequencing domain. For example, in machine learning for small molecule therapeutics, **the chemical structure is the spectral property that separates molecules into structurally dissimilar train–test splits**^68."
- Methods, p.1519: the protocol — "To generate a SPC, we create splits with SPs between 0 and 1 in 0.05 increments. For each SP, we generate **three splits with different random seeds**." 80–20 train–test.
- Methods, p.1521: an explicit diagnostic for "your split axis is not the whole story" — "SPECTRA can detect whether there exists an unconsidered spectral property. This occurs if **large variations exist in model performance in splits generated with the same SP** or if there is a **positive slope in the shape of the SPC** (that is, model performance improves with decreasing cross-split overlap)."
  ▸ Directly transferable to us: an auc_norm > 1 (retention improving with noise) is the same red flag, and our script already treats it as one (scripts/generate_paper_figures_v2.py:1853–1863, threshold 1.05).

A3. Representation choice versus model choice — what they actually say
There is **NO explicit representation-vs-architecture variance statement** in this paper. What exists is adjacent and quotable in two forms:
- Architecture-attributed robustness, with a mechanism (p.1516): "This correlation is **model specific**; ESM2 performance experiences no degradation in performance as diff-RRDR increases in the SP = 0.9, 0.95 and 1.0 splits (Fig. 4e). **ESM2 considers a larger range of input positions (512) than the CNN (12) when making a prediction. We expect this to make ESM2 more invariant** to increases in diff-RRDR."
  ▸ This is the template for how a robustness-difference-by-architecture claim is made acceptable: name the structural property of the architecture (receptive field) that causes the invariance. They do not stop at "architecture matters".
- Input-side (representation/pretraining) attribution (p.1517): "We calculated the overlap between these task-specific datasets and UniRef50, the pretraining dataset used by ESM2 … finding a **significant correlation between this overlap and the AUSPC of ESM2 (Spearman rank correlation 0.9, P = 1.4 × 10⁻²⁷)**", and it replicates across four other foundation models (Spearman 0.9, P = 0.04, p.1517).
- Model ranking is explicitly refused (p.1516): "**No single model outperforms others across all tasks by AUSPC** (Fig. 3b)." And p.1518: "**The purpose of this study is not to rank models by their AUSPCs** but to demonstrate SPECTRA on state-of-the-art models. To rank models requires averaging over many AUSPCs calculated across various tasks in a SPECTRA benchmark for molecular sequencing data."
- The paper's own cited pointer to the rep-vs-model literature is ref. 68, p.1522: **Dias, A. L., Bustillo, L. & Rodrigues, T. "Limitations of representation learning in small molecule property prediction." Nat. Commun. 14, 6394 (2023).** NOT READ (not in the supplied set) — but this is the single most on-point citation for our headline claim and should be obtained.

C) CONVENTIONS FOR REPORTING A FACTORIAL MODEL × REPRESENTATION SWEEP

Visual/tabular forms actually used in this set:
1. **Full-factorial table, one row per (model or kernel) × representation, one column per dataset, mean ± standard error over N random trials, blanks where a cell could not be computed.** GAUCHE Tables 1–4, PDF p.6: "RMSE values for 80/20 train/test split across **20 random trials**"; "Errorbars represent the **standard error** across 20 random initialisations" (§4.1, PDF p.6). Failed cells left blank and the reason stated: "For the SELFIES representation, some molecules could not be featurised and corresponding entries are left blank" (PDF p.6); Appendix C, PDF p.16: "Numerical errors were encountered with the WL kernel on the large lipophilicity dataset which invalidated the results and so the corresponding entry is left blank. The native random walk kernel was discontinued (for the time being) due to poor performance!" — i.e. **honest holes are conventional; imputed or averaged-over holes are not.** This is the convention that covers our GP/gauche gap.
2. **One table per metric, never a composite score.** GAUCHE reports RMSE (Table 1), NLPD (Table 2), and defers MSLL (Table C2) and QCE (Table C3) to appendix (PDF p.6, p.16).
3. **Performance curve per model, plus a scalar area summary.** Nature MI Fig. 2b–d (p.1515), Fig. 3 (p.1516).
4. **Bar plot of the scalar with SD error bars**, floor anchored to a null: Fig. 3 caption, p.1516 — "b, **Average AUSPC across all models and tasks. The y axis begins at random AUSPC.** Error bars in both plots indicate standard deviation." ⚠ AVERAGING FLAG: this is an average over models AND over tasks; it hides which task drives a model's score, and the paper itself immediately warns about that (p.1518).
5. **Distribution of within-cell standard deviation as a diagnostic panel** — Nature MI Fig. 4a, p.1517: "Distribution of standard deviation of test metric performance across splits generated with the same spectral parameter … Splits with a high standard deviation could have new spectral properties." This is a directly reusable idea: plot the spread of our 10 replicates per (model, rep, strategy, σ) cell as its own panel rather than burying it in an error bar.
6. **Trial-level distributions shown, not just means**: GAUCHE Fig. 3, PDF p.8 — "BO performance reporting the standard error from 50 randomly initialised trials (20 for Buchwald-Hartwig). **A kernel density estimate over the trials is shown on the right axis.**"

Statistical treatment expected:
- **Variance decomposition / ANOVA: NOT the convention in this set. Neither Nature MI nor GAUCHE runs one.** NO SOURCE FOUND for an ANOVA in any of the three papers. Our ANOVA is therefore an *addition* to convention, not conformity to it — it will be read as a claim needing its own justification (design, exclusions, residual interpretation), not as a box-tick.
- **Paired tests: NOT used in this set.** NO SOURCE FOUND (no Wilcoxon, Friedman, critical-difference diagram, or paired t-test in any of the three PDFs).
- **Rank correlation IS the convention for linking a covariate to performance.** Nature MI p.1516: "Spearman's rank correlation of ρ = −0.51, P = 1.79 × 10⁻⁵, between diff-RRDR and AUROC"; p.1517: "Spearman rank correlation 0.9, P = 1.4 × 10⁻²⁷" and "Spearman rank correlation 0.9, P = 0.04". Figure captions state "All correlations shown are significant (P < 0.05)" (Fig. 5 caption, p.1517).
- **Concordance across metrics is asserted qualitatively, not tested.** GAUCHE PDF p.6: "One trend to note is that uncertainty estimate quality is **roughly correlated** with regression performance"; PDF p.7: "**BO performance is tightly coupled to regression and UQ performance.**" Neither is accompanied by a coefficient.
- **The nearest thing to a rank-concordance convention** is in the similarity review, p.7: "since there are many alternatives to measure similarity (or distance) …, Miranda-Quintana et al. have proposed the **Differential Consistency Analysis**. It was found that the consensus between Tanimoto and the Cosine coefficients improved by choosing a query whose similarity to the rest of the molecules varies less, or by describing the molecules in a manner that does not depend strongly on their size." (Ref [3] = Miranda-Quintana, Bajusz, Rácz, Héberger, "Differential consistency analysis: which similarity measures can be applied in drug discovery?", Mol. Inf. 40(7), 2021 — p.19 of the review.) NOT READ.
- **Explicit refusal to rank without adequate averaging** — Nature MI p.1518, quoted in A3. Practical consequence for us: a reviewer schooled on this paper will accept "no single model wins everywhere" far more readily than "model X is more robust", and will demand the disaggregation before any ranking.

D) STRONGEST COUNTER-ARGUMENTS TO "ARCHITECTURE MATTERS MORE THAN REPRESENTATION FOR NOISE ROBUSTNESS"

Ranked by how damaging they are.

D1. **The factor levels are not comparably spanned, so the comparison is of ranges, not of factors.** This is the deepest objection and none of these papers answers it. An ANOVA effect size for "model" versus "rep" is a statement about the particular 6-or-so models and the particular set of representations we chose; widen the representation set (add a learned/pretrained embedding, or a degenerate one) and the effect size moves. GAUCHE's Table 1 (PDF p.6) is the concrete exhibit: the representation range there (Scalar Product on photoswitch: 22.5 → 36.6 RMSE) dwarfs the model range at fixed representation (20.9 / 22.5 / 20.9). A reviewer will say: your representations are all information-rich fingerprint/descriptor variants; you excluded the levels that would have made representation dominate.

D2. **Representation and model are not orthogonal factors in this field, so "which matters more" is ill-posed.** GAUCHE PDF p.6: "the best choice of representation (**and hence the choice of kernel**) is task-dependent." Kernel/architecture is *chosen for* the representation (Tanimoto for bit vectors, RBF for continuous, WL for graphs — GAUCHE §3, PDF pp.4–5; RBF for RXNFP vs Tanimoto for DRFP/OHE, PDF p.7). If in our sweep some model × rep cells are natural pairings and others are forced, the interaction term is confounded with pairing quality and the main effects are not cleanly interpretable.

D3. **Task/dataset dependence — the effect may not survive a second task.** Nature MI p.1516: "No single model outperforms others across all tasks by AUSPC"; abstract p.1512: "deep learning models consistently show reduced performance, **varying by task and model**." GAUCHE PDF p.6: task-dependence of the best representation. If our architecture-dominance holds on QM9 and on KIRBy/validation but the magnitudes differ, we must show both, not the pooled result.

D4. **Data-regime dependence.** GAUCHE PDF p.8: "most studies concluded that simple one-hot encodings (OHE) perform at least on par with more elaborate molecular representations **in the low-data regime**." And PDF p.1: "For small datasets, however, deep neural networks are often not the model of choice (Tom et al., 2022)." A reviewer will ask whether architecture-dominance is really an n-dependence in disguise. This collides with the already-flagged supervisor concern about the Fig 8 "smaller datasets" claim (paper_insertion_dataset_size_effect).

D5. **No mechanism = no claim.** Nature MI p.1516 does not merely assert that architecture matters; it names the causal property — receptive field 512 vs 12 positions — and shows the invariance follows. Our claim needs the analogous statement: *what property of the architecture* (ensembling / smoothing / explicit noise variance term / regularisation strength) produces the robustness, and does the ordering of models by that property match the ordering by auc_norm?

D6. **Model-specific noise handling makes "robustness" partly a definitional artefact for some models.** GAUCHE §2.1, PDF p.2: GP hyperparameters "comprise kernel hyperparameters, θ, in addition to **the likelihood noise, σ_y²**", and "Iσ_y² represents the variance of i.i.d. Gaussian noise on the observations y", fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood. A GP given Gaussian label noise will absorb it into σ_y² by construction; a model with no such term cannot. Under our Gaussian strategy that is a structural advantage that is not "robustness" in the sense the paper claims. ⚠ This also means the six strategies are not interchangeable evidence for the claim — the ones with non-Gaussian per-molecule structure (Quantile, Outlier: per-molecule spread 20× and 30×) are the ones that actually test it.

D7. **Protocol sensitivity of the baseline itself.** Everything in Nature MI (pp.1513–1514) says the split determines the number. Since auc_norm is normalised by R²(σ=0), and R²(σ=0) is a scaffold-split number, a shift in split protocol shifts every retention curve's denominator. A reviewer can ask whether architecture-dominance is stable under a harder split, and we have no answer unless we run one.

E) REPORTING A ROBUSTNESS / DEGRADATION-CURVE METRIC

E1. Is an area-under-a-degradation-curve established? **Yes, the area-under-the-curve form is established; the *normalisation by baseline* is not, in this literature.**
- Nature MI introduces exactly this metric class: abstract p.1512 — "SPECTRA plots model performance as a function of decreasing cross-split overlap and **reports the area under this curve as a measure of generalizability**"; p.1513 — "We propose the area under this curve (AUSPC) as a new, more comprehensive metric for model generalizability"; Fig. 1 caption p.1514 — "The area under the spectral performance curve (AUSPC) (highlighted in yellow) **summarizes model performance across all levels of cross-split overlap** and is a new metric for model generalizability."
- ▸ Our robustness curve is R²(σ) vs σ; theirs is metric vs cross-split overlap. Structurally identical: a stressor axis, a performance axis, an integral. **Cite AUSPC as precedent — it is the strongest available.**
- They do **not** divide by the σ=0 value. They handle the baseline problem differently: Fig. 3 caption, p.1516 — "**The y axis begins at random AUSPC.**" I.e. anchor the floor at chance, do not rescale by the model's own ceiling.
- They flag the metric as provisional: p.1518, Future directions — "**considering metrics beyond the area under the curve to summarize the SPC**."
- NO SOURCE FOUND in GAUCHE or the similarity review for any area-under-degradation-curve metric.

E2. What our metric actually is (so the paper states it correctly)
scripts/generate_paper_figures_v2.py:1791–1800:
```
def _retention_auc_norm(sig, r2, base):
    ret = r2 / base
    srange = sig.max() - sig.min()
    return float(trapezoid(ret, sig) / srange) if srange > 0 else np.nan
```
i.e. the σ-averaged retention fraction R²(σ)/R²(0), trapezoid rule, divided by the σ range. Docstring, same lines: "Primary robustness scalar. HIGHER = more robust (≈[0,1]); decoupled from baseline performance (unlike raw AUC or the old NDS slope). No shape assumption — just the mean fraction of baseline R² retained across σ."
⚠ NDS FLAG: anywhere paper.tex still says NDS / noise degradation slope, it is describing a metric no longer computed (v1 `generate_paper_figures.py` and `results/paper_figures/` are the stale NDS artefacts). Not audited in this task — separate pass needed.

E3. What a reviewer will want before trusting auc_norm
- **Precedent cited.** AUSPC (Nature MI, p.1513) — say plainly that we adopt the area-under-degradation-curve form and state the one difference: we normalise by R²(0) where SPECTRA anchors the axis at chance.
- **Justification for the denominator, and a sensitivity check without it.** Dividing by R²(0) makes the metric unstable when R²(0) is small — which is exactly why the script needs `ROBUSTNESS_BASELINE_THRESHOLD` exclusions (generate_paper_figures_v2.py:1803, 1815–1838) and the validation-side artefact filter `VALIDATION_AUC_LOW/HIGH` (lines 1460–1473). Every exclusion must be reported as a count and a list, in the GAUCHE style of leaving cells blank with a stated reason (Appendix C, PDF p.16), not silently dropped.
- **Out-of-range values reported, not suppressed.** auc_norm > 1 means performance improved under noise. The script already warns at 1.05 (lines 1853–1863). Nature MI treats the same phenomenon as diagnostic, not as noise — Methods p.1521: "if there is a **positive slope in the shape of the SPC** (that is, model performance improves with decreasing cross-split overlap)" indicates an unconsidered property. Report these cells and say what they mean.
- **The curve behind the scalar, for at least a representative set of cells.** Nature MI shows SPCs (Figs 2b–d p.1515, Fig 3 p.1516) alongside AUSPC. A scalar with no curve invites "your integral is hiding a non-monotonic or divergent tail."
- **Sampling of the stressor axis stated.** Nature MI Methods p.1519: "splits with SPs between 0 and 1 in **0.05 increments**", 3 seeds each. We need the equivalent sentence: which σ grid, how many replicates, and — because trapezoid weights the endpoints half — whether the result is sensitive to the σ range chosen. Our σ range choice is load-bearing given the experimental-noise anchor (σ=0.6 ≈ one unit of real assay error).
- **Concordance with a second, simpler robustness readout** (e.g. R² retained at the single physically-anchored σ). Both GAUCHE (PDF pp.6–7) and Nature MI assert metric agreement qualitatively; giving an actual Spearman ρ between auc_norm and the point measure is cheap, matches the Spearman convention in Nature MI (pp.1516–1517), and pre-empts "your metric is doing the work."
- ⚠ AVERAGING FLAG on the metric itself: auc_norm is already a mean over σ within a cell. It must not be additionally averaged across the 6 strategies, across the 10 replicates, or across representations in any headline number — and the paper should say in words that each reported auc_norm is a per-(model, rep, strategy) quantity, because a reader who has read Nature MI p.1518 knows that averaging is where ranking claims go wrong.
