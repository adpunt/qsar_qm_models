## B) FIVE MOST RELEVANT — READ PROPERLY

**1. Karimi et al. 2020, `1-s2.0-S1361841520301237-main.pdf`**
The paper reviews label-noise handling in deep learning, then runs its own experiments on three medical imaging datasets, and closes with per-category recommendations. It is explicit that it concerns *label* noise only, not measurement noise: "we are only interested in label noise, and not data/measurement noise... Although x_i may include measurement noise, that is not the focus of this review" (pdftotext lines ~145–150) — that is exactly our scope and is worth citing to defend it. Two things bear hard on us. First, it sorts remedies into six categories (label cleaning, architecture change / noise layers, robust loss, data re-weighting, data-and-label consistency, training-procedure change) and reports that **which one wins is task-dependent**: "it is likely that for each application one has to experiment with a number of methods to find the most suitable one" (line ~1567). That is a survey-level version of our own Kendall's-W finding, but pointing the opposite way — they find method ranking is *not* stable across tasks, we find model ranking *is* stable across noise strategies (`paper.tex:433`, W = 0.9121). Worth confronting rather than ignoring. Second, and directly threatening: their concluding limitations note says label noise and *worst-case / outlier* performance are "essentially a different problem" (line ~1548). Our outlier noise strategy sits precisely on that seam, and it is the strategy where our residual term hits 83.6% (`paper.tex:380`). They also report concrete degradation magnitudes that are useful as scale anchors: Tanaka et al.'s joint-optimization method "improved the classification accuracy on CIFAR-10 by 2% with low label noise rate to 17% when label noise rate was very high" (line ~788), and Tanno et al.'s annotator-confusion method gave "improvements of 8–11%" (line ~508).

**2. Boulougouri, Vandergheynst & Probst 2024, `s42256-024-00856-0.pdf` — Nature Machine Intelligence 6:754–763**
They replace the molecular graph with an unordered *set* of atom invariants and learn over it with RepSet-style set encoders (MSR1/MSR2). Their central result is deflationary and lands squarely on our representation argument: MSR1, with "no explicit topological information about the molecular graph", "performed better than D-MPNN in 5 out of 11 benchmark datasets and better than GIN in 8 out of 11", from which they conclude "this may suggest that up to now, too much value has been assigned to representing a molecule as a graph rather than a loose set of atoms" (pdftotext lines ~620–625). They then immediately hedge that this "may be due to the nature of the benchmark datasets" (line ~625). They use Murcko scaffold splits throughout (line ~618), matching our protocol (`paper.tex:197`). For us this is corroborating evidence for the "representation matters less than you think" half of our scientific-contribution claim (`paper.tex:167`) — but note it is a *clean-label* result, so it supports our claim on a different axis than we do, and we should say so rather than let it read as independent confirmation of a noise finding.

**3. Fang et al. 2022, `s42256-021-00438-4.pdf` — Nature Machine Intelligence 4:127–134 (GEM)**
GEM adds 3D geometry (bond lengths/angles) to a GNN plus geometry-level self-supervised pretraining. It reports SOTA on "14 out of 15 datasets", with "an overall relative improvement of 8.8% on average" on regression and "4.7% on the average ROC-AUC" on classification (pdftotext lines ~862–869), and the geometry-only ablation (GeoGNN, no pretraining) gives "7.9% compared with the best results of previous methods" (line ~890). It uses MoleculeNet with scaffold split, calling scaffold split "more challenging" and better for generalization (lines ~369–371). **This is the strongest published counter-evidence to our headline.** GEM's whole thesis is that representation choice buys large, reproducible gains — which is compatible with our result only because our ANOVA measures variance share within *our* roster, and GEM-class geometric representations are not in that roster (`paper.tex:203` lists ECFP4, SNS, PDV, OHE-SMILES, mol2vec, MHG-GNN — none geometric). Our conclusion should be stated as "within the representation families evaluated here", not as a general law, or a reviewer holding GEM will reject the claim. Note also their improvements are largest on regression tasks tied to quantum-chemical properties (line ~868) — which is our QM9 setting exactly.

**4. Boiko et al. 2025, `s42256-025-01031-9.pdf` — Nature Machine Intelligence (stereoelectronics-infused molecular graphs, SIMG)**
They build graphs from natural bond orbital (NBO) calculations, then learn a cheap approximation (SIMG*) that avoids DFT+NBO at downstream training time. **They benchmark on QM9 and specifically on HOMO–LUMO gap — our exact reference task** (`paper.tex:164`). They state "Our representation outperforms all others for highest occupied molecular orbital–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital gap prediction" against SOAP and SPAHM baselines, and the learned SIMG* "displays virtually the same performance as the original SIMG" (pdftotext lines ~1552, ~1573). They also report a clean ablation separating *features* from *topology*: "SIMGs with either atomic features or topology provide a comparable improvement over both the molecular graph and ChemProp baselines... Full SIMGs yield an even bigger improvement" (lines ~1569–1571). Bearing on us: this is a second clean-label demonstration that representation choice moves QM9 gap prediction substantially, and it normalizes its metrics to chemical accuracy — an absolute reference scale we do not use. It does not touch noise, so it does not pre-empt our robustness result; it pre-empts any *performance* claim about representation on QM9 gap.

**5. Wang, Ye, Xie, Tang & Fan 2024, `HSALC_hard_sample_aware_label_correction_for_medic.pdf` — Multimedia Tools & Applications**
HSALC splits training data into three sets — clean, **hard**, and noisy — using training difficulty and loss, then deliberately *injects extra noise into the already-clean subset* and re-applies the division criterion to verify the split, before training a correction model to relabel the noisy set. Their claim is that performance gains are largest "especially with high noise ratios" (abstract, page 1). Two direct bearings. First, the clean/hard/noisy trichotomy is the concept our outlier and heteroscedastic strategies are missing: those strategies concentrate the noise dose on statistical outliers (which are also the hard samples), so "model can't fit it" and "label was corrupted" are not separable in our design — which is a plausible mechanistic story for why residual, not model, dominates those two strategies (`paper.tex:380`: 83.6% and 77.4%). Second, their re-injection-to-verify trick is methodologically close to what NoiseInject already does mechanically, and is a defensible citation for the validity of controlled re-injection as a diagnostic.

## C) 🚩 PAPERS THAT ALREADY ANSWER OUR RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Our three stated questions are at `/Users/apunt/repos/qsar_qm_models/paper.tex:188`: (1) contributions of representation vs. model architecture to performance and noise robustness; (2) probabilistic vs. deterministic robustness and whether per-sample uncertainty tracks corrupted labels; (3) generalization of noise-robustness patterns across noise mechanisms and properties.

**🚩🚩 CRITICAL — Cortes-Ciriano, Bender & Malliavin 2015 substantially pre-empts Question 1 and Question 3.**
No PDF of it is in Downloads, but it is quoted verbatim in `Uncertainty Quantification in QSAR Modeling...pdf` (lines 99–118). Its own framing is: *"To date, no systematic study has assessed the effect of random experimental errors on the predictive power of QSAR models. To address this shortage, we have benchmarked the noise sensitivity of 12 learning algorithms on 12 data sets (15,840 models in total)"* (line 104–106), with *"noise... simulated by sampling from Gaussian distributions with increasingly larger variances"* (line 108–109). Full factorial: 12 algorithms × 12 datasets × 10 noise levels (line 112). Reported key finding: *"Algorithm ranking changes with noise level—no single best algorithm across all noise conditions"* (line 115). **This directly contradicts our headline stability result** (`paper.tex:433`: Kendall's W = 0.9121, "a model's relative ranking of robustness is largely strategy-independent") and it claims the "first systematic study" ground. Our defensible differentiators are that they vary *datasets* while we vary *noise mechanism* (six strategies vs. Gaussian only), and that they hold representation fixed while we cross model × representation. **This must be cited and explicitly distinguished in the introduction, or the novelty claim at `paper.tex:167` is not survivable in review.** Note also the arithmetic: 12×12×10 = 14,400, not 15,840 — verify against the original before quoting.

**🚩 Koutsoukas et al. 2017 (J. Cheminform. 9:42) pre-empts part of Question 1 for classification.**
Quoted in the same compilation (lines 119–133): *"when exploring robustness to noise, non-linear methods were found to perform well when dealing with low levels of noise, lower than or equal to 20%, however when dealing with higher levels of noise, higher than 30%, the Naïve Bayes method was found to perform well and even outperform at the highest level of noise 50% more sophisticated methods"* (lines 123–126). Also: *"Random Forest consistently robust across moderate noise levels (<30%)"* (line 132) — that is our RF finding (`paper.tex:164`) already in print, though for classification-with-label-flipping rather than regression-with-additive-σ.

**🚩 Kolmar & Grulke 2021 (`s13321-021-00571-7.pdf` — in your skip list) pre-empts our clean-test-set design rationale.**
Quoted at compilation lines 77–98. They already ran Gaussian noise injection at "up to 15 levels" across "8 datasets with 6 QSAR endpoints" and 5 algorithms including GP, and already concluded that GP's ability to ingest measurement uncertainty is "a unique feature of the GP algorithm" (line 86–89). Our Question 2 partially overlaps. Since another agent has this file, I flag only the collision.

**Non-collision but adjacent:** Dong & Rudin (`s42256-020-00264-0.pdf`) does not answer our questions but undermines how we *phrase* the answer — if many near-equally-accurate models disagree about importance, an η² attributing variance to "model" vs "representation" is a property of the roster, not of QSAR. Our own roster is already pruned by ρ > 0.99 exclusion (`paper.tex:262`), which makes the roster-dependence concrete and citable.
